# Coffee keeps the stories, loses the records

Your thesis is right about the symptom and wrong about the cause. Green coffee does not lack producer story. **61.1% of stocked Purveyors listings already carry a substantial farm narrative, averaging about 1,256 characters.** What it lacks is a persistent, verifiable producer identity that a track record can build on. Only **17.8%** of those listings carry any structured producer identity. Only **10 of 417** farm names match exactly across suppliers. And **2,529 delisted listings** hold narratives attached to nothing (first-party Purveyors data, see caveats below). Wine's advantage was never a better story. It was roughly 170 years of legally enforced, persistent identifiers (appellations, château names, parcel registers, later LWIN codes), against which prices, critic scores and vintages could pile up. In hedonic studies, wine prices track that accumulated reputation more closely than blind sensory quality. Buffett is the right comparison, but for a sharper reason than "look at the full picture." His qualitative judgments were almost all about _durability_, and he read durability from long quantitative records. Later research showed most of his "full picture" could be turned into hard data. The thesis also misses four things: producer context is mostly a credence good, so it is worthless unless verified; story measurably changes how coffee tastes; producer storytelling coexists with producers capturing only **7 to 16 cents of each extra specialty dollar**; and in much of coffee the producer is not a single person. For the blog, the line is "coffee has stories, not records." For the product, the move is an entity-and-claims layer, the natural extension of ADR-004's disclosure and provenance model to producers and farms.

## Wine built a register first and told stories later

Wine's context layer did not come from culture. It came from fraud. Phylloxera destroyed **over 40% of French vines** between the late 1850s and mid-1870s, and the shortfall was filled with raisin wine, sugar wine and wine made without grapes ([Wikipedia: Great French Wine Blight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_French_Wine_Blight)). Growers revolted. Languedoc protests started in 1893 and peaked in 1907. In 1911, Champagne rioters attacked the warehouses of firms "known to produce fake Champagne" ([Champagne Riots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Champagne_Riots); [Languedoc revolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolt_of_the_Languedoc_winegrowers)). The legal answer came in about 30 years: an origin law in 1905, a protection law in 1919, and the appellation body that became INAO in 1935 ([Wikipedia: AOC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appellation_d%27origine_contr%C3%B4l%C3%A9e)). The ordering matters for Purveyors. **Truth about origin came first. Quality hierarchies and critic scores were layered on top decades later.**

These institutions mostly guarantee identity, not quality. AOC label rules are enforced "to the millimetre," while in-bottle quality control is lax. Germany's 1971 law registers about 2,600 single vineyards but ranks none of them. US AVAs certify only that at least 85% of the grapes come from the named area ([AOC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appellation_d%27origine_contr%C3%B4l%C3%A9e); [German classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_wine_classification); [AVA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Viticultural_Area)). Even the famous Bordeaux 1855 classification is a broker's ranking by "reputation and trading price." It has changed only twice in 170 years, and it classifies châteaux whose vineyards have since "expanded, shrunk and been divided" ([1855 classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bordeaux_Wine_Official_Classification_of_1855)). Burgundy's **1,247 delimited climats**, each its own legal parcel, took from 11th-century Cistercian ownership to UNESCO listing in 2015 to settle ([Climats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climats,_terroirs_of_Burgundy)). What lasts is the stable identifier, not the accuracy of any ranking.

The price evidence shows why stable identity matters. Combris, Lecocq and Visser found that Bordeaux market prices are "essentially determined by the objective characteristics" on the label, while blind jury grades are driven by sensory characteristics. Price and tasted quality run on different variables ([Economic Journal 1997](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ecj/econjl/v107y1997i441p390-402.html)). A meta-regression of more than 180 hedonic wine models finds only a **moderate +0.30 partial correlation between price and sensory quality**. Its headline is "the relative importance of a wine's reputation over its sensory quality," with an important qualifier: producers must sustain quality over time to earn the return ([Oczkowski & Doucouliagos 2015](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/ajagec/v97y2015i1p103-121..html)). **Reputation is not the opposite of quality. It is the stored history of quality.** That history becomes cash at public checkpoints. Parker's grades were worth about **€2.80 per bottle** in en primeur prices, a figure identified through a natural experiment when his 2003 publication slipped past price-setting ([Ali, Lecocq & Visser 2008](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ecj/econjl/v118y2008i529pf158-f173.html)). Vintage quality is largely predictable from weather ([Ashenfelter 2008](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ecj/econjl/v118y2008i529pf174-f184.html)).

The modern data layer followed the same logic. Liv-ex's LWIN gives each wine a free, Creative Commons code that is independent of vintage. Vintage, bottle size and pack size are appended as children, so offers and tasting notes from many merchants and many years collect on one entity ([Liv-ex LWIN](https://www.liv-ex.com/lwin/)). Wine-Searcher, the largest aggregator, wired LWIN into its search and merchant feeds in 2022 and called it "the equivalent of an ISBN number for books" ([Wine-Searcher](https://www.wine-searcher.com/m/2022/08/wine-searcher-integrates-lwin-wine-codes)). Wine's lesson for coffee is a three-layer stack: a stable identifier, a repeated public signal against it, and a price history that reveals value over time. Coffee has a weak version of the first and almost none of the other two.

## Coffee already pays for identity, then throws it away

The coffee market already prices exactly what it fails to keep. In Cup of Excellence auctions, Traore, Wilson and Fields (2,123 lots) find that prices are "mainly determined by symbolic attributes and market conditions." Each score point adds about **18.68%**, but the effect is quadratic and turns _negative_ above **93.10**. Rank premiums dominate: **+133% for first place** relative to coffees outside the top four. Even the score itself is partly predicted by symbolic attributes such as country and certification ([Traore et al. 2018](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/18F08D9E4A9E6E03C46F9EF971ECBB8A/S1074070818000056a.pdf/what_explains_specialty_coffee_quality_scores_and_prices_a_case_study_from_the_cup_of_excellence_program.pdf)). Wilson and Wilson reach the same result: "the highest premiums stem from obtaining a top rank compared to other coffees from the same country" ([Agricultural Economics 2014](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ags/aaea13/150721.html)). The ordinary contract market shows it too. In the 2024 Specialty Coffee Transaction Guide (about 105,000 contracts), the 88+ point band had a median of $8.82/lb but an interquartile range of **$4.86 to $20.20**, about a 4× spread inside a single score band ([2024 SCTG](https://nzsca.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/2024TransactionGuideFinal-English.pdf)). Above roughly 86 points, the score stops explaining price, and something identity-shaped takes over.

At the extreme, named producers behave like grand cru estates. Hacienda La Esmeralda's Geisha sold for **$21/lb in 2004 and $601/lb in 2017**, and Lamastus's Elida lot reached $803/lb in 2018 ([Daily Coffee News](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2018/07/20/natural-geisha-breaks-best-of-panama-auction-record-at-803-per-pound/)). A 2025 Esmeralda lot reportedly sold for $30,204/kg (about $13,700/lb) ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/annabel/2025/08/11/panamas-geisha-coffee-sets-a-record-price-its-source-is-worth-a-trip/)). _That figure is press-reported only; the auction record was not verified._ The premium sits on a persistent entity (farm plus variety plus competition history), not on any one harvest.

The strongest evidence comes from what happens when identity is removed. In December 2008 the Ethiopia Commodity Exchange began trading coffee "in a commodity fashion" that "eroded traceability." The price gap between traceable and exchange-sourced coffee widened by **12.0 percentage points**. That cost an estimated **$280M, or 26% of farmers' coffee income** ([Leung 2014](https://cri-world.com/publications/qed_dp_249.pdf); a discussion paper with 699 transactions, not peer-reviewed). It is a sharp story for the post. Within about 18 months, Ethiopia trademarked its regional _names_ (Sidamo, Yirgacheffe and Harar, ending in the June 2007 Starbucks licensing deal) and then commoditized its _lot identity_ ([WIPO](https://www.wipo.int/en/web/ip-advantage/w/stories/the-coffee-war-ethiopia-and-the-starbucks-story); [CSRwire](https://csrwire.com/press-release/oxfam-celebrates-win-win-outcome-for-ethiopian-coffee-farmers-and-starbucks/)). The protected regional name did not make up for the lost lot identity. A coffee GI is an appellation without the estate layer above it.

Purveyors' own catalog shows the same failure from the inside. _These are first-party figures from a read-only production query on 2026-10-02. I have not independently re-run them. The site and farmer extraction fields are only about seven months old, so part of the gap is backfill state, not supplier behavior._ Across 45 suppliers and 2,674 stocked listings (8,956 historical), **61.1% carry substantial farm narrative but only 17.8% carry any structured producer identity, and only 1.2% name a farmer in a structured field.** Across all history, 417 distinct farm or site names appear on 831 listings. Only 10 match exactly across more than one supplier: Ratnagiri Estate at four suppliers, Kayon Mountain Farm and Los Pirineos at two. Near-duplicates such as "La Esperanza" and "Granja La Esperanza," or "Ana Maria" and "Ana María Donneys," go unlinked. The result is the reverse of wine. Coffee writes the tasting note and the estate history fresh for every offer, then discards both when the lot sells out. Nobody in coffee has published a panel linking farm identities across harvests. No study measures whether a farm's cup score or auction rank persists year to year. The only evidence of repeat winners is roaster marketing, such as claims that Fazenda Samambaia topped Cup of Excellence in 2000, 2001 and 2014 ([Dabov](https://dabov.us/our-farmers)). That missing study is itself a finding. The data infrastructure needed to ask the question does not exist.

## Buffett read durability from records, not stories

The Buffett comparison holds, but the popular version ("evaluate the whole business, not the ticker") undersells it. His acquisition criteria ask for "demonstrated consistent earning power (future projections are of little interest to us, nor are 'turnaround' situations)" ([1987 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1987.html)). His canonical moat is defined by a hard metric: "an enduring 'moat' that protects excellent returns on invested capital" ([2007 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2007ltr.pdf)). The qualitative judgment is real, and he calls the moat test "more subjective," but its object is durability. The evidence for durability is a long numeric record. See's Candies is the model: bought for $25M while earning 60% pre-tax on capital, it produced **$1.35 billion of cumulative pre-tax earnings** on $32M of reinvestment, with unit volume growing "only 2% annually." Buffett credits a "durable competitive advantage, built by the See's family over a 50-year period" ([2007 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2007ltr.pdf)). A family's decades of work showed up as a quantitative signature. That is the coffee analogy. **A producer's "context" is most Buffett-like when it is longitudinal: consistency across harvests, tenure of buyer relationships, repeat appearance across importers.** A richer one-off biography is not the analogy. Buffett explicitly discounted forward-looking narrative, which is exactly what aspirational farm copy offers.

Two more Buffett ideas sharpen the post. First, "Good jockeys will do well on good horses, but not on broken-down nags." When a brilliant manager takes on a business with bad economics, "it is the reputation of the business that remains intact" ([1989 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1989.html)). A great producer in a structurally weak position, such as climate exposure, a thin export channel or a single buyer, is still a fragile supplier. Context has to include structural position, not just character. Second, circle of competence: "knowing its boundaries, however, is vital" ([1996 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1996.html)). A roaster does not need every fact about every origin. They need decision-relevant context and an honest signal of where the data runs out. For the "ticker shapes" point, cite Mr. Market's "incurable emotional problems" ([1987 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1987.html)). _Do not use the widely shared "turned the charts upside down" quote; no primary source for it was found. Also note that "Price is what you pay; value is what you get" is Buffett quoting Graham ([2008 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2008ltr.pdf))._

The most useful non-obvious point comes from Frazzini, Kabiller and Pedersen. Berkshire's alpha "becomes insignificant when controlling for exposures to Betting-Against-Beta and Quality-Minus-Junk factors." A systematic portfolio of cheap, safe, high-quality stocks "performs comparably to Berkshire Hathaway" ([NBER w19681](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w19681/w19681.pdf); [FAJ 2018](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2018/faj-v74-n4-3)). Buffett's "full picture" was not ineffable. It left fingerprints in hard data that academics could later score. His genius was "recognizing early on... that these factors work," plus about 1.6 to 1.7× cheap leverage from insurance float. **This reframes your thesis. The coffee context gap is mostly a data-structure problem, not a "numbers can't capture it" problem.** It also exposes a choice the post has to make. Once a quality signal is hardened and widely published, it becomes a factor, not an edge. Purveyors can sell context as private edge to individual roasters, or as market efficiency (fairer pricing, less lemons discounting). It cannot fully be both.

The theory of hard and soft information explains why coffee loses context in the first place. Liberti and Petersen define soft information as information "difficult to completely summarize in a numeric score, where context matters," that must be "collected in person." Hardening it into a score always loses something ([NBER w25075](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25075/w25075.pdf)). Stein shows that hierarchies do well only when information can be "costlessly 'hardened' and passed along" ([Journal of Finance 2002](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v57y2002i5p1891-1921.html)). The green coffee chain runs producer, then mill or co-op, then exporter, then importer, then roaster. Each handoff adds hierarchical distance, and what survives is the hard fields: origin, process, score, price. **A cupping score is soft information already hardened.** Liberti and Petersen's test, "Your interpretation of a 3 must be the same as mine," is the exact calibration problem with scores from different importers, which the SCTG itself flags ([2024 SCTG](https://nzsca.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/2024TransactionGuideFinal-English.pdf)). There are two warnings in the same literature. Nineteenth-century credit bureaus' private detailed reports predicted business failures better than their published ratings. And when securitization passed along only hard data, lenders stopped collecting soft data and credit models deteriorated ([w25075, citing Carruthers and Rajan, Seru & Vig](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25075/w25075.pdf)). A marketplace that ranks only on hard fields teaches suppliers to optimize those fields and stop gathering the rest.

## What the thesis misses: verification, distortion, value capture and the unit of identity

**Producer context is mostly a credence good.** Origin and process are search attributes only if they are true. Cup quality is an experience attribute that sample roasting can check. Farm identity, practices, labor and price paid to the producer are credence attributes, which a roaster cannot taste. Darby and Karni showed that credence markets invite fraud, and Akerlof's lemons paper ends on "counteracting institutions" such as brands and licensing ([Darby & Karni 1973](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jlawec/v16y1973i1p67-88.html); [Akerlof 1970](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/qjecon/v84y1970i3p488-500..html)). Coffee's record bears this out. The Kona complaint alleged that about **2.7 million pounds of real Kona is grown each year while more than 20 million pounds is sold as Kona**. Settlements reached at least $33.4M; plaintiffs' counsel cites more than $41M, and the gap between the two figures is unresolved ([Daily Coffee News](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2023/09/27/federal-judge-approves-final-12-million-settlement-in-kona-labeling-case/); [Lieff Cabraser](https://www.lieffcabraser.com/2023/10/court-grants-final-approval-to-settlement-in-kona-coffee-litigation/)). WCR DNA testing found that **only 39% of 88 samples labeled Gesha were genetically Gesha**, mostly from nursery confusion rather than fraud. WCR sells the test ([Daily Coffee News](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2020/04/08/genetic-testing-reveals-the-true-nature-of-coffee-varieties/)). Wine is no better off where verification is weak. Rudy Kurniawan sold tens of millions of dollars of fakes and was caught partly by producers who knew their own records. Domaine Ponsot showed it never made Clos Saint-Denis before 1982, yet he was offering 1945–1971 vintages ([Wikipedia: Kurniawan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudy_Kurniawan)). **What protects buyers is records held by the producer, not a richer narrative.**

**Story changes the coffee.** Sörqvist and colleagues served identical coffee in two cups and falsely labeled one "eco-friendly." People who cared about sustainability preferred its taste, paid more for it, and kept paying more after being told they had preferred the other cup ([PLOS ONE 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0080719)). Plassmann's fMRI study shows a higher stated price raises both reported pleasantness and activity in the brain's pleasure-encoding region ([PNAS 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2242704/)). In a Brazilian study, consumers given information about a fermented specialty coffee noticed citrus, while uninformed tasters noticed caramel ([SciELO](http://www.scielo.br/j/cta/a/cSjz6CM9ScRVL6KHypQdyYy/?lang=en)). Wine's Judgment of Paris is the counter-case: a _blind_ tasting that re-rated California ([Blind wine tasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blind_wine_tasting)). The Heirloom Cacao Preservation Fund builds the fix into its process. Submissions are processed blind for an expert panel before any designation, and identity is then anchored genetically by USDA-ARS ([HCP](https://www.hcpcacao.org/en/news/first-ever-heirloom-cacao)). Whether _named-producer_ stories specifically bias trained roasters is unstudied. Applying the label studies to them is an inference.

**More story has not meant more money for producers.** Wienhold and Roberts find that across differentiated specialty segments, people in exporting countries "only absorbed between 7 and 16 cents of each additional dollar." Low-end specialty FOB prices are "little more than 13% of retail," compared with 23.5% for coffee overall. All of this coexists with "prolific references to specific coffee producers" in marketing. The authors name "unequal access to information" as a cause ([Daily Coffee News summary of World Development 2025](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2025/07/15/a-closer-look-at-value-distribution-in-specialty-coffee-markets/)). The 2026 Coffee Barometer found that none of 15 large roasters and traders disclosed pricing structures, contract durations or premiums: "Opacity, the evidence shows, is a choice" ([Daily Coffee News](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2026/07/14/inside-the-2026-coffee-barometer-part-2-what-companies-are-not-saying/)). Critics also note that specialty volume concentrates on "a remarkably small circle of highly visible, competition-winning farms" while being marketed as hidden gems ([Coffee Intelligence](https://intelligence.coffee/2026/01/specialty-coffee-and-producers-success/)). Relationships do not reliably raise prices either. The best-identified relationship-coffee study (265 Colombian smallholders, propensity-score matching) found that "farm gate prices did not significantly differ," though participants gained access to credit ([Hernandez-Aguilera et al.](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ags/aaea15/205650.html)). **The strongest objection to the thesis is that coffee's real gap is commercial terms, not biography.** A platform that adds narrative without terms could make "story instead of distribution" worse.

**The producer is often the wrong unit, and the producer often does not own the data.** Coffee comes from about **12.5 million farms, 95% of them under 5 ha** ([Fairtrade Risk Map](https://riskmap.fairtrade.net/commodities/coffee)). Smallholders supply 60–67% of output, depending on the source ([CARTO/Enveritas](https://carto.com/blog/enveritas-coffee-poverty-visualization/); [Coffee Intelligence](https://intelligence.coffee/2023/12/smallholder-coffee-farmer/)). A fully traced Ethiopian lot usually means a washing station, not a farmer ([Ozone Coffee](https://ozonecoffee.co.uk/blogs/journal/ethiopia-coffee-trading-system)). Terroir science points the same way. Altitude is the one consistently positive variable across a 73-study review ([Frontiers in Plant Science 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2021.708013/full)). Field work finds that slope aspect, yield load, harvesting and post-harvest practice "play a predominant role" ([Avelino et al. 2005](https://www.gem.university/wp-content/uploads/documents/pdf/environment/agroforestry/fx_slope_aspect_altitude_coffee_quality_costs_rica_avelino_2005.pdf)). Coffee terroir is more local and more about practice than wine's regional story, so the meaningful entities are the farm, the station and the practice. And coffee metadata leans heavily on altitude, the one variable that is easy to state. Ownership is the bigger blind spot. Wine and Comté worked because producers or their collectives owned the name. Comté's farmers typically own the fruitières they sell to ([culture magazine](https://culturecheesemag.com/travel/comte-cheese/)). In coffee, the importer owns the listing and the story. Solidaridad warns that farmer data tools "are often controlled by external actors... creating dependency" ([Solidaridad](https://www.solidaridadnetwork.org/publications/empowering-coffee-farmers-with-digital-data-management/)). EUDR will make importers collect plot geolocation from 30 December 2026. But under the 2025 revision, downstream operators keep only a reference number, so roasters will not receive richer producer data ([EU Access2Markets](https://trade.ec.europa.eu/access-to-markets/en/news/delay-until-december-2026-and-other-developments-implementation-eudr-regulation)). The industry's blockchain traceability flagship, Farmer Connect, ended up as EUDR compliance software after Agridence acquired it in 2025 ([Food Ingredients First](https://www.foodingredientsfirst.com/news/agridence-farmer-connect-acquisition.html)). That suggests the bottleneck is capturing and matching entity data, not tamper-proofing it.

## Implications for the post and for Purveyors product decisions

**For the blog post**, the argument that holds up is: _coffee has stories, not records._ Open with the Purveyors numbers (six in ten listings carry a farm story, fewer than two in ten carry a structured producer identity, and the stories disappear when the lot sells out). Then contrast wine, where the identifier came first and the reputation accumulated against it. Use Buffett for the durability argument: See's 50-year family record showing up as returns on capital, "future projections are of little interest," and the Frazzini result that the full picture was largely hardenable. Use Ethiopia's ECX as the cost of losing identity. Make three concessions that strengthen the piece: verification beats volume, story changes taste, and context without commercial terms can serve the roaster more than the farmer. _Do not claim_ that direct trade raises farm-gate prices, that producer names raise consumer willingness to pay (stated-preference evidence covers origin labels, not named producers), or that wine labels guarantee quality. The coffee-app repo already has an outline titled ["What If Coffee Transparency Is a Database Schema Problem?"](https://github.com/reedwhetstone/coffee-app/blob/main/notes/blog/outlines/coffee-transparency-schema-problem.md) built on ADR-004. This post should be framed as the identity and track-record sequel, not as a repeat of the schema argument.

**For the product**, ADR-004 already sets the precedent. It separates disclosed, inferred and not-disclosed processing claims, records confidence and evidence, and refuses to turn missing into "none" ([ADR-004](https://github.com/reedwhetstone/coffee-app/blob/main/notes/decisions/004-processing-transparency-schema-api.md)). The producer layer should apply the same contract one level up. The table maps each borrowed principle to a concrete move.

Order the work the way wine did. **First, entity resolution.** Fold accents, turn prefixes like Finca, Granja and Hacienda into a site-type attribute, separate personal names from farm names, block candidates on country and region before fuzzy matching, and keep an alias table with provenance. This alone would link pairs like "La Esperanza" and "Granja La Esperanza." **Second, the track record.** Every new season's listing re-observes the farm, so the scraper effectively serves as Purveyors' renewal mechanism. Building it yields the coffee equivalent of a wine vertical: one farm across harvests and suppliers, with price, score and descriptors over time. That is the persistence study the literature lacks, and it is the most defensible "Buffett" feature. **Third, the guardrails.** Treat disclosure of commercial terms as a first-class field where suppliers provide it. Never publish plot geolocation. Record attribution and consent status for narrative text scraped from importers. Do not let ranking surfaces amplify the same celebrated farms. Two cheap internal tests should come before heavy investment. One is a Combris-style regression of Purveyors prices on identity fields versus cupping scores. The other checks how much of the 17.8% coverage gap closes once the seven-month-old extraction fields finish backfilling.

## Conclusion

The deepest lesson from both comparisons is that context only builds up when it has somewhere to attach. Wine's reputation premium, Buffett's moat judgments and credit bureaus' scores all depended on a durable entity observed again and again over time. The story was what people read off that record, not a substitute for it. Coffee has turned this upside down. It produces story in volume, at the leaf level, and discards it on sale. That makes Purveyors' position unusual. As a cross-supplier aggregator it sits where Wine-Searcher sat when LWIN arrived. It may be the only actor that sees the same farm through 45 importers' eyes, which is exactly the vantage needed to turn ephemeral narratives into a longitudinal record.

There is also an unraveling dynamic to aim for. Once disclosing producer identity becomes cheap, structured and verifiable, a missing identity stops being neutral and starts to signal something, the way an unexplained gap in a company's filings does for an investor. The ADR-004 pattern of disclosed, inferred and not-disclosed is the mechanism that makes silence informative. The risk is the one Buffett's replicators expose: a hardened signal stops being anyone's private edge. So the honest pitch is not "context gives you an edge." It is "records make the market fairer, and fairer markets reward the producers who sustain quality."
