# How Wine Built Its Foundation for Producer and Place Context, and Evidence That Context (vs. Sensory Quality) Drives Value

Research note for the Purveyors.io "coffee producer context gap" post. Compiled 2026-10-02. Search was rate-limited (Brave HTTP 429) for much of the session, so several primary texts (INAO decrees, EU Reg. 2019/33, Landon & Smith full text) could not be read directly. Those items are listed under Gaps. They are not filled in from memory.

## 1. What institutions made place and producer context persistent in wine, what do they require, and when did they arise?

### Takeaway

Wine's context layer is not a cultural mood. It is about 170 years of stacked, legally enforced institutions: broker price rankings (1855), fraud-driven origin laws (1905 to 1919), a public-private appellation body (1935), parcel-level vineyard registers (Burgundy's 1,247 climats; Germany's roughly 2,600 Einzellagen), and copycat systems abroad (US AVAs, 1980). Together they make "where and who" a legally protected, label-mandatory attribute. Most of these institutions control truth-in-origin, not sensory quality.

### Cited Findings

**Bordeaux 1855 classification (a codified price and reputation ranking, not a tasting)**

- Napoleon III asked for a classification for the 1855 Exposition Universelle in Paris. Wine-trade brokers ranked the wines "according to a château's reputation and trading price" into first through fifth growths. The broker list is dated 18 April 1855. — [Wikipedia: Bordeaux Wine Official Classification of 1855](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bordeaux_Wine_Official_Classification_of_1855)
- The list has changed only twice: Cantemerle was added in 1856, and Mouton Rothschild was elevated to first growth in 1973 "after decades of intense lobbying." A 1960 revision attempt failed. — [same](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bordeaux_Wine_Official_Classification_of_1855)
- The 1855 ranking classifies châteaux (brands), not land. Estate vineyards have "expanded, shrunk and been divided without any reclassification." Any parcel inside the commune can be used by a classified château regardless of the parcel's quality. Critics argue the list is outdated as a quality guide. — [same](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bordeaux_Wine_Official_Classification_of_1855)

**French origin law (built in response to fraud)**

- The first French law on viticultural designations of origin is dated 1 August 1905. The Law of 6 May 1919 for the Protection of Appellations of Origin specified the region and commune of production. — [Wikipedia: AOC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appellation_d%27origine_contr%C3%B4l%C3%A9e)
- The Comité National des appellations d'origine (CNAO) was created on 30 July 1935 by government and major growers, on Joseph Capus's initiative. It became the public-private INAO after WWII. The AOC seal was mandated by laws in the 1950s through the 1970s. INAO's scope extended beyond wine on 2 July 1990. — [Wikipedia: AOC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appellation_d%27origine_contr%C3%B4l%C3%A9e)
- What AOC requires: production and processing within a defined area using recognized traditional methods, under a product-specific standard. Selling a non-compliant product under an AOC name is illegal, and no part of an AOC name may appear on a non-qualifying label. Producers are legally required to state place of origin. — [Wikipedia: AOC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appellation_d%27origine_contr%C3%B4l%C3%A9e)
- Scale: over 300 French wine AOCs. In 2018, 47% of French wine production was AOC wine. AOCs range from Côtes du Rhône (about 30,000 ha, 171 villages) to Château-Grillet (under 4 ha). — [Wikipedia: AOC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appellation_d%27origine_contr%C3%B4l%C3%A9e)
- Weakness: label rules are enforced "to the millimetre," but in-bottle quality control is lax. The approval tasting often happens before bottling, by local experts who may have ties to vintners. — [Wikipedia: AOC, citing Joseph 2005](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appellation_d%27origine_contr%C3%B4l%C3%A9e)
- Spain moved first: Rioja was regulated in 1925 and Sherry in 1933. — [Wikipedia: AOC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appellation_d%27origine_contr%C3%B4l%C3%A9e)
- The EU PDO/PGI system now harmonizes geographical indications. Wines may keep using "AOC" on labels. Germany's classification was reorganized under the EU wine market organization from 1 August 2009. — [Wikipedia: AOC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appellation_d%27origine_contr%C3%B4l%C3%A9e); [Wikipedia: German wine classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_wine_classification)

**Burgundy climats (parcel-level place identity, UNESCO 2015)**

- "Climats, terroirs of Burgundy" was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2015 (39th session, ref. 1425, criteria iii and v). It covers 13,219 ha plus a 50,011 ha buffer zone. — [Wikipedia: Climats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climats,_terroirs_of_Burgundy); [UNESCO 1425](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1425/)
- There are about 1,247 climats: small, precisely delimited parcels, each with its own AOC definition. Many boundary walls and paths date from 1938. The parceling traces to 11th-century Cistercian ownership and 12th- and 13th-century bourgeois ownership. The concept of named climats, each with its own wine, developed in the 16th century. — [Wikipedia: Climats, citing ICOMOS 2015 evaluation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climats,_terroirs_of_Burgundy)

**German Prädikat system (ripeness-based, not site-based)**

- Germany's 1971 Wine Law introduced the system. Nearly all vineyards are registered as one of about 2,600 Einzellagen. Any vineyard can produce any quality level if grape must weight reaches the minimum, so "quality" means grape ripeness alone, and vineyards are not ranked. — [Wikipedia: German wine classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_wine_classification)
- The private producer association VDP (about 200 estates, organized since 1910) compensates with its own Grosse Lage and Erste Lage vineyard ranking, based on 19th-century Prussian tax maps. — [same](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_wine_classification)
- German labels routinely carry producer, vintage, village plus vineyard, variety, Prädikat, and region. — [same](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_wine_classification)

**US American Viticultural Areas (origin truth, no method rules)**

- An AVA label requires at least 85% of grapes from the AVA, with the wine fully finished in the AVA's state. A state appellation requires 100% from that state. — [Wikipedia: AVA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Viticultural_Area); [Wikipedia: AOC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appellation_d%27origine_contr%C3%B4l%C3%A9e)
- ATF began defining AVAs in 1980. Augusta (Missouri) was the first AVA, on 20 June 1980. TTB took over in 2003. There are currently about 280 AVAs in 34 states, 154 of them in California. — [Wikipedia: AVA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Viticultural_Area)
- AVA petitions (27 CFR 9.12) must show name recognition, a boundary rationale, a narrative of distinguishing features (climate, geology, soils, elevation), and USGS maps. — [Wikipedia: AVA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Viticultural_Area)
- South Africa's Wine of Origin scheme (1973) likewise regulates labelling accuracy but not varieties, yields, or irrigation. — [Wikipedia: AOC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appellation_d%27origine_contr%C3%B4l%C3%A9e)

### Inferences

- The common denominator across France, Germany, the US, and South Africa is legally verifiable origin disclosure on the label, at a defined granularity (region, then commune, then vineyard). Production-method rules (France) and ripeness rules (Germany) are add-ons that vary by country. For coffee, the analog to copy is a verified, persistent identifier for farm or lot plus producer, not a quality grade.
- The two most famous hierarchies encode different things. Bordeaux 1855 encodes a brand's market price in 1855. Burgundy encodes the land parcel. Both persist because the identifier stays stable over time, which lets track records accumulate against it.
- Systems are often messy and only loosely tied to quality: 1855 is "outdated," Prädikat ignores site, and the AOC tasting is weak. Their value comes from making identity persistent and comparable, not from being accurate quality measures.

### Gaps

- Primary texts were not retrieved: INAO decrees (1935 décret-loi), EU Regulation 1308/2013, and EU Delegated Regulation 2019/33 on mandatory label particulars such as bottler or producer name. Exact current EU label requirements for producer and bottler disclosure are unverified here.
- No source was retrieved for Napa Valley AVA's establishment date (commonly cited as 1981), the first published vintage charts, or Burgundy's 19th-century vineyard rankings (e.g., Lavalle 1855). These are omitted.

## 2. Hedonic price evidence: reputation, appellation, producer, vintage, and critic scores vs. sensory quality

### Takeaway

The best-established finding: in hedonic models, label and reputation attributes explain market price better than blind sensory quality. Sensory quality is still positively priced, but only moderately (meta-analytic partial correlation of +0.30), and long-run reputation matters more than current quality. Critic scores carry measurable cash value: Parker's grade was worth about €2.80 per bottle en primeur. Weather-driven vintage quality is priced, and it is predictable from public information.

### Cited Findings

- **Combris, Lecocq & Visser (1997), _Economic Journal_ 107(441):390–402.** They estimated a hedonic price equation for Bordeaux including both label ("objective") attributes and jury-graded sensory attributes. "The market price is essentially determined by the objective characteristics" on the label, while jury quality grades are "essentially determined by the sensory characteristics." In other words, price and blind-tasted quality are driven by different variables. — [RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ecj/econjl/v107y1997i441p390-402.html)
- **Oczkowski & Doucouliagos (2015), _American Journal of Agricultural Economics_ 97(1):103–121.** This meta-regression covers over 180 hedonic wine price models across 20 years and many countries. The price–sensory-quality relation is a "moderate partial correlation of +0.30," even though consumers lack quality information and expert tasters are inconsistent. They found no publication bias. Heterogeneity is explained by "the importance of a wine's reputation," use of the 100-point scale, single-variety samples, and functional form. The authors' headline implication is "the relative importance of a wine's reputation over its sensory quality": producers must sustain quality over time to earn returns. Producer reputation does not change the strength of the price–quality link, but "this finding does not contradict the importance of wine producer reputation in directly influencing prices." — [RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/ajagec/v97y2015i1p103-121..html)
- A related summary of the meta-analysis says that correcting for attenuation bias in quality ratings increases the price–quality partial correlations. — [ResearchGate listing](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/281139311_Wine_Prices_and_Quality_Ratings_A_Meta-regression_Analysis)
- **Landon & Smith.** "The Use of Quality and Reputation Indicators by Consumers: The Case of Bordeaux Wine," _Journal of Consumer Policy_ 20 (1997) — [MPRA 9283](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/9283/). Also "Quality Expectations, Reputation and Price," _Southern Economic Journal_ 64(3):628–647 (1998). The task brief said _JPE_; the correct journal is _SEJ_. — [UAlberta record](https://ualberta.scholaris.ca/items/934c719f-279c-4d5c-bb28-da281b6b2076). The SEJ paper frames itself as quantifying firm reputation effects, which "few empirical studies" had done. — [Brave snippet of UAlberta record](https://ualberta.scholaris.ca/items/934c719f-279c-4d5c-bb28-da281b6b2076). Specific coefficients were not retrieved (see Gaps).
- **Ali, Lecocq & Visser (2008), "The Impact of Gurus: Parker Grades and En Primeur Wine Prices," _Economic Journal_ 118(529):F158–F173.** In 2003 Parker published his grades in autumn, after en primeur prices were set, instead of in spring. Using this natural experiment, the authors estimate a Parker effect of **€2.80 per bottle** on average. — [RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ecj/econjl/v118y2008i529pf158-f173.html)
- **Ashenfelter (2008), "Predicting the Quality and Prices of Bordeaux Wine," _Economic Journal_ 118(529):F174–F184.** Variability in Bordeaux vintage quality and prices "is predicted by the weather that created the grapes." The real return to holding wine is about 2–3% per year. The weather equation implies far greater variability in en primeur prices than is observed, which suggests early prices under-react to fundamentals. The paper provides a basis for assessing "the role of expert opinion in determining wine prices." — [RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ecj/econjl/v118y2008i529pf174-f184.html)

### Inferences

- The decomposition supported by evidence: market price is mostly predicted by label identity (appellation, classification, château, vintage), plus a moderate, real sensory-quality component (r ≈ 0.30), plus a discrete critic-information premium (≈ €2.80 per bottle), plus a vintage or weather component.
- The Oczkowski & Doucouliagos conclusion that "producers need to sustain the sensory quality of a wine over time to extract appropriate returns" is the key nuance for Purveyors. Reputation is accumulated quality history, not quality's opposite. Context data (producer and lot track records) is valuable because it lets past quality evidence be priced. That supports the "track record" framing over the "story" framing.
- The Combris finding transfers directly to a coffee test. Regress green coffee prices on listing metadata vs. cupping scores, and see whether price tracks identity or cup.

### Gaps

- Landon & Smith's specific magnitudes (often summarized as long-run reputation having a much larger price effect than current quality) could not be verified from accessible text, so no number is given.
- R² values for Combris et al. and for Ashenfelter's weather equation were not in the retrieved abstracts.
- No retrieved source quantifies the appellation or classification premium (e.g., first-growth vs. fifth-growth price multiple) in a peer-reviewed hedonic model.

## 3. Critics, en primeur, vintage reports, and verticals in building producer track records

### Takeaway

Critic scores and en primeur pricing turn reputation into an annual, public, priced signal. Parker's spring grades measurably moved en primeur prices. Ashenfelter shows vintage quality is largely forecastable from weather, which weakens the case that critics add unique information. Some evidence suggests markets under-react to vintage fundamentals early on.

### Cited Findings

- Parker's grades are normally published in spring before en primeur prices are set, so they enter the price-discovery process. The 2003 timing reversal identifies a causal effect of about €2.80 per bottle. — [Ali, Lecocq & Visser 2008](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ecj/econjl/v118y2008i529pf158-f173.html)
- En primeur prices show less variability than weather fundamentals imply. Holding returns are about 2–3% real per year. — [Ashenfelter 2008](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ecj/econjl/v118y2008i529pf174-f184.html)
- Market-based reclassification persists alongside the official one. Liv-ex publishes a price-based "Liv-ex Bordeaux Classification" (2009). Alexis Lichine campaigned for revision for over 30 years and published his own unofficial classification. — [Wikipedia: 1855 classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bordeaux_Wine_Official_Classification_of_1855)
- Use of the 100-point scale is one of the study characteristics that shifts estimated price–quality correlations across hedonic studies. — [Oczkowski & Doucouliagos 2015](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/ajagec/v97y2015i1p103-121..html)

### Inferences

- Wine's track-record machinery has three layers: a stable identifier (château or climat), a repeated annual signal (vintage plus critic score), and a secondary market that reveals price over time (auction, Liv-ex). Coffee has a weak identifier (lots change names and importers), a one-shot signal (single cupping score at offer), and almost no secondary price history. Purveyors' multi-supplier catalog history could become the coffee equivalent of a vertical: the same farm across harvests and suppliers.

### Gaps

- No primary source was retrieved on the history of vintage charts, on how verticals (multi-vintage tastings) are used in valuation, or on the current state of en primeur after Parker's retirement. These are not covered.

## 4. Counterpoints: blind tasting, price expectations, fraud, terroir skepticism, and story substituting for quality

### Takeaway

Strong, replicated evidence shows that context changes perceived taste. Price labels raise both reported pleasantness and neural pleasure signals. Labels and color override sensory description. Blind, non-experts don't prefer pricier wine. Expert judges are inconsistent. Fraud cases show high-context markets reward provenance claims enough to make forgery lucrative, and buyers may not detect fakes by taste. Context drives value, but it can be gamed and can partly decouple from the liquid.

### Cited Findings

- **Goldstein, Almenberg, Dreber, Emerson, Herschkowitsch & Katz (2008), _Journal of Wine Economics_ 3(1):1–9.** In more than 6,000 blind tastings, the price–rating correlation is "small and negative," so people on average enjoy more expensive wines slightly less. People with wine training show "a non-negative relationship." Results are robust to individual fixed effects and trimming. — [RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jwecon/v3y2008i01p1-9_00.html)
- **Plassmann, O'Doherty, Shiv & Rangel (2008), PNAS 105(3), doi:10.1073/pnas.0706929105.** Subjects in an fMRI scanner tasted wines they believed were different and differently priced. "Increasing the price of a wine increases subjective reports of flavor pleasantness as well as [BOLD] activity in medial orbitofrontal cortex." — [Europe PMC abstract for DOI 10.1073/pnas.0706929105](https://europepmc.org/search?query=DOI:10.1073/pnas.0706929105)
- **Brochet / Morrot, Brochet & Dubourdieu (2001), "The Color of Odors," _Brain and Language_ 79(2):309–320.** 54 Bordeaux oenology students described a white wine dyed red with red-wine descriptors and did not detect that both glasses were the same wine. In a separate Brochet test, one mid-range Bordeaux was served under a grand cru label ("woody, complex, round") and under a table-wine label ("short, light, faulty"). — [Wikipedia: Blind taste test, citing Morrot et al. 2001 and Brochet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blind_wine_tasting)
- **Hodgson (JWE 2008, 2009).** At the California State Fair competition, the same wine was served to judges three times without their knowledge, and the results showed judges were inconsistent. Across competitions, medals appeared "distributed at random." Hodgson calls objective evaluation of large numbers of wines "beyond human ability," while conceding that individual exceptional tasters exist. — [Wikipedia: Blind taste test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blind_wine_tasting) (secondary; precise percentages not retrieved)
- **Quandt & Ashenfelter (1999, _Chance_)** re-analyzed the 1976 Judgment of Paris and found low inter-taster consistency. The Judgment itself, a blind tasting, is credited with launching California's fine-wine prestige, a case where removing context changed reputations. — [Wikipedia: Blind taste test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blind_wine_tasting)
- **Wiseman (2011, not peer-reviewed).** 400 members of the public could not distinguish expensive from inexpensive wines. — [Wikipedia: Blind taste test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blind_wine_tasting)
- **Rudy Kurniawan fraud.** He consigned at Acker Merrall in 2006, with sales netting $10.6M and $24.7M (the latter a record for a single wine sale). He was caught partly by producers who knew their own production records: Le Pin flagged fake 1982 magnums in 2007, and Laurent Ponsot showed in 2008 that the domaine never made Clos Saint-Denis before 1982, yet Kurniawan offered 1945–1971 vintages. He also offered more magnums of 1947 Lafleur than were produced. The FBI arrest on 8 March 2012 found cheap Napa wine with notes for passing it off as old Bordeaux, plus corks and labels. He was convicted in December 2013, sentenced to 10 years on 7 August 2014, and released in November 2020. As many as 10,000 fakes may remain in collections (per the _Sour Grapes_ documentary). After deportation, guests at Singapore dinners reportedly preferred his fakes "on multiple occasions." — [Wikipedia: Rudy Kurniawan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudy_Kurniawan) (the post-deportation claims rest on press reports, so treat them as anecdotal)
- **Terroir-as-marketing critique (partial).** The 1855 ranking is a price and reputation snapshot that persists despite land changing hands. The AOC quality check is weak relative to label enforcement. Germany's legal system ignores site entirely. — [Wikipedia: 1855](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bordeaux_Wine_Official_Classification_of_1855); [Wikipedia: AOC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appellation_d%27origine_contr%C3%B4l%C3%A9e); [Wikipedia: German classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_wine_classification)

### Inferences

- These findings don't refute "context drives value." They show the mechanism: context shapes both willingness to pay and the experienced product (Plassmann). The ethical and commercial line is whether context is verified. Kurniawan was ultimately caught by producer-held production records (which vintages and how many bottles exist). That argues verified provenance data, not narrative, is the durable asset.
- Story can substitute for quality at the margin, as the Brochet and Plassmann effects show. But the meta-analysis says sustained returns require sustained quality, so story without a track record is fragile.
- Contested vs. established: the price-expectation effect (Plassmann, Brochet) and expert inconsistency (Hodgson) are well established. The extent to which terroir is real sensory signal vs. marketing is contested and not resolved by the sources here. Goldstein's expert vs. non-expert split suggests trained buyers (like green coffee roasters) do get some sensory value from price.

### Gaps

- No peer-reviewed study was retrieved that directly tests whether terroir is detectable blind. The Plassmann wine prices ($5/$45, $10/$90 as commonly cited) and Hodgson's exact consistency percentages were not verified here.
- No 2024–2026 updates were found on wine fraud enforcement or provenance tech (e.g., NFC/blockchain capsules), because search was rate-limited.

## 5. How long did it take wine to build this, and what was the trigger?

### Takeaway

Parcel identity in Burgundy took centuries (Cistercians in the 11th century, named climats in the 16th). The legal infrastructure was compressed into about 30 years (1905 to 1935). It was triggered by phylloxera, then the fraud and adulteration it spawned, then grower revolts over fake wine and outside grapes. Origin law was a fraud-prevention and economic-protection response, not a marketing project.

### Cited Findings

- Phylloxera (the "Great French Wine Blight") devastated over 40% of French vines from the late 1850s to the mid-1870s. Production of "cheap raisins and sugar wines" filled the gap and caused problems that outlasted the blight. — [Wikipedia: Great French Wine Blight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_French_Wine_Blight)
- In the Languedoc, the market was partly supplied by wine from imported dried grapes, watered wine, chaptalized wine, and "wines" made without grapes. Southern winemakers demanded suppression of sugaring in 1892. A Montpellier demonstration on 12 December 1893 gathered thousands of signatures against fraud. The 1907 revolt was repressed by Clemenceau's government. The four southern departments produced about 40–45% of French wine. The same source notes fraud may have been no more than 5% of the market, though growers blamed it heavily. — [Wikipedia: Revolt of the Languedoc winegrowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolt_of_the_Languedoc_winegrowers)
- Champagne: in 1908 the government announced it would delimit the Champagne area by decree. That decision included the Marne and parts of the Aisne and excluded the Aube. Growers were hit by phylloxera (15,000 acres destroyed in one year), losses of nearly 96% of the crop, and suspicion that merchants used non-Champagne grapes. In January 1911 riots broke out: rioters dumped Loire grapes into the Marne, attacked warehouses of firms "known to produce fake Champagne," and shouted "A bas les fraudeurs." The final settlement included the Marne, Aube, and parts of the Aisne. — [Wikipedia: Champagne Riots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Champagne_Riots)
- Legal timeline: origin-designation law of 1 August 1905, protection law of 6 May 1919, CNAO on 30 July 1935, INAO after WWII, AOC seal mandated across the 1950s through 1970s. — [Wikipedia: AOC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appellation_d%27origine_contr%C3%B4l%C3%A9e)
- Elsewhere: Rioja (1925) and Sherry (1933), German Wine Law (1971), South Africa Wine of Origin (1973), US AVAs (1980). — [Wikipedia: AOC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appellation_d%27origine_contr%C3%B4l%C3%A9e); [Wikipedia: German classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_wine_classification); [Wikipedia: AVA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Viticultural_Area)
- Burgundy: Cistercian and bourgeois parceling (11th–13th centuries), the climat concept (16th century), boundary walls (1938), UNESCO (2015). — [Wikipedia: Climats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climats,_terroirs_of_Burgundy)

### Inferences

- Wine's context system shows punctuated buildup: centuries of informal place reputation, then a roughly 30-year burst of law (1905–1935) forced by an external shock (phylloxera, then adulteration, then grower violence), then about 50 years of international diffusion (1925–1980). Critic-score and secondary-market layers (Parker, Liv-ex) arrived only in the late 20th century.
- Coffee has partial analogs: Colombia's protected designation, Jamaica Blue Mountain, and Kona labeling fights. Coffee has had no equivalent fraud-plus-crisis trigger that forced granular, producer-level origin law. For a private platform the implication is that the infrastructure can be built voluntarily through data rather than legislation. But wine's history suggests incumbents adopt it when mislabeling threatens their prices.
- Note the order: origin truth (anti-fraud) came first, and quality hierarchies and critic scores were layered on afterward. A coffee context layer would plausibly need verified identity and provenance before story.

### Gaps

- No source was retrieved on how long specific producer reputations took to form, or on the price evolution of classified growths before and after 1855.
- Coffee-side analogs (Kona 1990s mislabeling, Colombia PDO 2007) were not researched here. They are out of scope for this note and were not verified.
