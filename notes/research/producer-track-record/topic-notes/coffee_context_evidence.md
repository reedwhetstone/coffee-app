# Producer, farm, and place context in specialty green coffee: what drives value, what is disclosed, why context fails to accumulate

Research date: 2026-10-02. Researcher notes for Purveyors.io blog/thesis ("coffee has metadata but lacks durable context"). Search provider was rate-limited intermittently; several paywalled sources (Wiley, ScienceDirect) could only be read at abstract level — flagged where relevant.

---

## Q1. Hedonic price studies: how much do sensory score vs. country/region/producer reputation explain specialty coffee prices?

### Takeaway

Every major Cup of Excellence (CoE) hedonic study finds sensory score matters, but that rank, country of origin, variety, lot size and other "symbolic"/reputation attributes explain as much or more of price. The most recent large study (Traore, Wilson & Fields 2018, n≈2,123 lots) concludes that prices are "mainly determined by symbolic attributes and market conditions." The return to score is nonlinear and even turns negative at the very top, so the score alone does not settle price.

### Cited Findings

- **Donnet, Weatherspoon & Hoehn (2007 working paper; 2008 _Agricultural Economics_ 38(3):267–76):** CoE e-auction prices are "influenced by the quality rating, which is a sensory variable, and by the quality rankings established in the cupping competition previous to the auction, the country of origin and the coffee variety, which are reputation variables." Harvest year and the commodity price were also significant. — [RePEc abstract, MSU Staff Paper 9310](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ags/midasp/9310.html); journal citation via [Traore et al. 2018 references](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/18F08D9E4A9E6E03C46F9EF971ECBB8A/S1074070818000056a.pdf/what_explains_specialty_coffee_quality_scores_and_prices_a_case_study_from_the_cup_of_excellence_program.pdf)
- Donnet et al. (2008) found Pacamara was the only variety with a significant price effect. — [Traore et al. 2018, p. 362–363](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/18F08D9E4A9E6E03C46F9EF971ECBB8A/S1074070818000056a.pdf/what_explains_specialty_coffee_quality_scores_and_prices_a_case_study_from_the_cup_of_excellence_program.pdf)
- **Wilson & Wilson (2014, _Agricultural Economics_ 45(S1):91–105)**, CoE 2004–2010, replicating Donnet et al. with a truncated MLE model: "While sensory quality has a strong effect on price, **the highest premiums stem from obtaining a top rank compared to other coffees from the same country**, and North American buyers are more responsive to sensory quality than buyers in Asian and European markets." — [RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ags/aaea13/150721.html); [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/agec.12132)
- CoE auction prices average **4.5× the ICO composite price**, and growers receive **>80% of the final auction price**. — [Traore et al. 2018, p. 355, citing Wilson & Wilson 2014 and ACE](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/18F08D9E4A9E6E03C46F9EF971ECBB8A/S1074070818000056a.pdf/what_explains_specialty_coffee_quality_scores_and_prices_a_case_study_from_the_cup_of_excellence_program.pdf)
- **Traore, Wilson & Fields (2018, _Journal of Agricultural and Applied Economics_ 50(3):349–368)**, CoE data (2,123 lots in the main sample; flavor appendix covers 2004–2015). Key numbers, all from [the open-access PDF](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/18F08D9E4A9E6E03C46F9EF971ECBB8A/S1074070818000056a.pdf/what_explains_specialty_coffee_quality_scores_and_prices_a_case_study_from_the_cup_of_excellence_program.pdf):
  - Abstract: "material attributes are important determinants [of score], but symbolic attributes have a greater explanatory power. The hedonic price estimations show that specialty coffee prices are mainly determined by symbolic attributes and market conditions such as the number of coffees in the auction."
  - Each extra quality-score point raises price by **18.68%**. The effect is quadratic: above a score of **93.10**, further points have a _negative_ marginal effect. The authors cite cases "where higher-scoring coffees garnered lower prices than lower-scoring coffees in another auction."
  - Rank premiums over coffees outside the top four: **1st +133.08%**, **2nd +44.02%**, **3rd +26.71%**, **4th +15.06%**.
  - Each additional flavor descriptor in the cupping notes adds **+0.74%** to price. Specific descriptors: fruity +40.32%, sour acid +14.78%, floral +6.87%, sweet +3.75%, spice +3.20%, balanced +3.10%.
  - Organic certification adds **+14.95%**. Larger lots sell for less (–0.47% per unit of lot size), and each additional coffee in the auction lowers price by 0.76%.
  - Coffees from Bolivia, Colombia, Costa Rica, El Salvador, Guatemala and Mexico sell for more than Brazil's. Burundi and Rwanda are not significantly different from Brazil.
  - Even the **quality score itself** depends on symbolic attributes. Altitude, Pacamara (vs. Bourbon), Rainforest Alliance certification and country of origin all significantly predict score; certification is worth "almost an extra 1.5 points."
  - Conclusion: "buyers place more value on symbolic attributes than material attributes," and "a high-scoring coffee in one country may have the same or lower quality compared with a coffee from another country with a low score."
- **Teuber & Herrmann (2012, _Food Policy_ 37(6):732–40)** model regional (sub-national) origin with intercept and slope dummies. Using Brazil as the reference, Guatemala was rated a better-quality origin than Colombia and earned higher prices. They found Bourbon and Pacamara significant. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0306919212000930); [AgEcon record](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/126235/?ln=en); variety detail via [Traore et al. 2018](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/18F08D9E4A9E6E03C46F9EF971ECBB8A/S1074070818000056a.pdf/what_explains_specialty_coffee_quality_scores_and_prices_a_case_study_from_the_cup_of_excellence_program.pdf)
- **Teuber (2010, _J. of International Food & Agribusiness Marketing_ 22(3–4))**: data from US online retailers show single-origin coffees get significantly higher retail prices, with 100% Kona and Jamaica Blue Mountain the most expensive. — [Taylor & Francis abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/08974431003641612)
- The non-auction market shows the same pattern. In the **2024 Specialty Coffee Transaction Guide** (~105,000 contracts, >2 billion lb, Oct 2018–Sep 2024), 2023/24 FOB medians were: 80–81.9 pts $2.29/lb; 82–83.9 $2.65; 84–84.9 $3.02; 85–85.9 $3.61; 86–87.9 $4.38; 88+ **$8.82**. — [2024 SCTG PDF](https://nzsca.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/2024TransactionGuideFinal-English.pdf)
- Within a score band, price dispersion widens sharply with quality. The interquartile range was **$0.82** for 82–83.9 coffees ($2.28–$3.10) versus **$2.19** for 86–87.9 ($3.50–$5.69). For 88+ it ran **$4.86 to $20.20** in 2023/24. — [2024 SCTG PDF](https://nzsca.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/2024TransactionGuideFinal-English.pdf)
- The SCTG itself cautions that "coffee quality is subjective… data donors exhibit different biases when assessing coffee quality," and that some donors do not report scores at all. — [2024 SCTG PDF](https://nzsca.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/2024TransactionGuideFinal-English.pdf)
- Lot size: in 2023/24 the median for lots under 1,000 lb was **$4.78/lb**, almost double the **$2.59** for 40,000+ lb lots. — [2024 SCTG PDF](https://nzsca.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/2024TransactionGuideFinal-English.pdf)

### Inferences

- **Established:** score is necessary but not sufficient. Above roughly 86 points, most price variance comes from something other than the score: rank, origin reputation, variety (Gesha, Pacamara), scarcity/lot size, buyer market, and descriptors. That "something else" is mostly context about identity and reputation, which is the information the Purveyors thesis says fails to persist.
- The ~2–4× within-band spread at 88+ (SCTG) is consistent with reputation and relationship premiums. However, the SCTG does not decompose it, so this is an inference.
- Since CoE rank premiums are relative to coffees _from the same country_, the market prices position within a reference set. A durable producer entity with a track record is what makes that comparison legible outside a single auction.

### Gaps

- Could not access the full Donnet 2008 or Wilson & Wilson 2014 papers for R² values or exact score coefficients (paywalled; IFAMR PDF link expired). No published variance decomposition of "sensory vs. reputation share of price" was found.
- Wilson, Conley, Harris & Lafone (2012, _Applied Geography_ 35:499–507), "New terrains of taste: spatial analysis of price premiums for single origin coffees in Central America," is cited in Traore et al. but was not read; its findings are unverified here.
- No hedonic study tests a _named-producer_ fixed effect (producer reputation independent of score) in CoE data. That is the most direct test of the thesis, and none was found.

## Q2. Producer reputation premiums and repeat performance: does quality persist across harvests?

### Takeaway

Producer-level reputation clearly commands extreme premiums at the top (Hacienda La Esmeralda, Lamastus/Elida). However, no peer-reviewed study was found that measures whether a producer's quality or auction rank persists across harvests. The evidence for persistence is anecdotal or marketing copy.

### Cited Findings

- In 2004, Hacienda La Esmeralda (Peterson family) introduced Geisha at Best of Panama, and the winning bid was **$21.00/lb**. — [Daily Coffee News, 2018](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2018/07/20/natural-geisha-breaks-best-of-panama-auction-record-at-803-per-pound/)
- 2017: a Hacienda La Esmeralda Geisha sold for **$601/lb**, then the highest price ever paid for green coffee per pound. 2018: Lamastus Family Estates' "Elida Geisha Green Tip Natural" sold for **$803/lb**. That 2018 auction had 49 lots, mostly Geisha, nearly 6,000 lb in total, an average winning bid of **$98.68/lb**, and only nine lots under $50/lb. — [Daily Coffee News, 2018](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2018/07/20/natural-geisha-breaks-best-of-panama-auction-record-at-803-per-pound/)
- 2025: a Hacienda La Esmeralda Geisha lot reportedly sold for **$30,204/kg** (≈$13,700/lb) at the Best of Panama auction. — [Forbes, Aug 11, 2025](https://www.forbes.com/sites/annabel/2025/08/11/panamas-geisha-coffee-sets-a-record-price-its-source-is-worth-a-trip/) (press source; the full article and the auction platform record were not verified). UPI also reported it as a record ([UPI, Aug 8, 2025](https://www.upi.com/Top_News/World-News/2025/08/08/panama-geisha-coffee-record-price/6221754665895/), not fetched; 403).
- Anecdotal repeat winners, from roaster/importer marketing (low evidentiary weight): Fazenda Samambaia (Brazil) placed at the top of CoE in 2000, 2001 and 2014 ([Dabov](https://dabov.us/our-farmers)); Los Morales (El Salvador) won again after a 2021 win ([Coffeeness](https://www.coffeeness.de/en/cup-of-excellence/)).
- The rank premium in Q1 (1st place +133%) is earned within one harvest's competition. Each CoE lot is a single-harvest entity. — [Traore et al. 2018](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/18F08D9E4A9E6E03C46F9EF971ECBB8A/S1074070818000056a.pdf/what_explains_specialty_coffee_quality_scores_and_prices_a_case_study_from_the_cup_of_excellence_program.pdf)

### Inferences

- The Esmeralda/Panama Geisha case is the closest coffee analogue to a wine "estate" or "grand cru." Price escalated from $21/lb to $601/lb to roughly $13,700/lb, attached to a named farm and variety over two decades. The premium sits on a persistent entity (farm plus variety plus competition history), not on any single harvest's score.
- The _absence_ of a persistence study is itself informative. CoE and Best of Panama publish lot-level results by year, but nobody appears to have built a panel linking farm IDs across years. That is a data-infrastructure gap, and it is directly relevant to Purveyors.

### Gaps

- No peer-reviewed estimate of year-to-year autocorrelation in a farm's cup score or CoE rank. Building one would mean scraping CoE results (allianceforcoffeeexcellence.org) and matching farm names, which is exactly the entity-resolution problem in Purveyors' own data (10 of 417 farm names match across suppliers).
- Whether a prior CoE win raises a farm's _subsequent non-auction_ prices is unmeasured in the literature found.

## Q3. Coffee terroir science: altitude, shade, soil, microclimate, variety, processing

### Takeaway

**Established:** higher altitude is associated with better sensory scores, and excessive light exposure with worse ones. These are the most consistent findings across a 73-study systematic review. Slope exposure and yield load matter in field studies. **Contested or thin:** soil, CO₂, temperature and water-stress effects are inconsistent in direction. Variety effects are real but interact heavily with environment. Processing and harvest practice are repeatedly described as predominant, which means much "terroir" is actually producer practice.

### Cited Findings

- Systematic review of 73 articles (42 environmental, 20 management, 11 both). "The most consistent evidence… (1) increased altitude is associated with improved sensory attributes of coffee and; (2) increased light exposure is associated with decreased sensory attributes." Effects of CO₂, water stress and temperature showed inconsistent direction, flagged as research gaps. — [Ahmed et al. 2021, _Frontiers in Plant Science_](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2021.708013/full)
- **Avelino et al. 2005** (_J Sci Food Agric_ 85:1869–1876), two Costa Rican terroirs: Orosi (1,020–1,250 m) and Santa María de Dota (1,550–1,780 m). Findings: [Avelino et al. 2005 PDF](https://www.gem.university/wp-content/uploads/documents/pdf/environment/agroforestry/fx_slope_aspect_altitude_coffee_quality_costs_rica_avelino_2005.pdf)
  - East-facing slopes gave superior, more acidic cups: acidity 2.73/5 vs. 2.36 at Orosi, and 3.64 vs. 3.28 at Dota.
  - Taster preference rose with altitude in both terroirs.
  - Higher yield went with lower acidity at Dota.
  - The two terroirs had distinct flavor signatures (floral at Orosi, chocolate at Dota).
  - Caffeine, trigonelline, fat, sucrose and chlorogenic acid "were not well correlated with the sensory characteristics," so chemistry did not explain terroir differences.
- Avelino et al. also summarize prior literature: altitude has a positive effect and rainfall a negative one. "Among local techniques, the varieties cultivated, harvesting quality and postharvest processing play a predominant role," and shade is "reported as a factor favouring coffee quality" via light intensity and yield. — [same PDF](https://www.gem.university/wp-content/uploads/documents/pdf/environment/agroforestry/fx_slope_aspect_altitude_coffee_quality_costs_rica_avelino_2005.pdf)
- In CoE data, altitude significantly raises both quality score and price, and Pacamara significantly raises score vs. Bourbon. — [Traore et al. 2018](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/18F08D9E4A9E6E03C46F9EF971ECBB8A/S1074070818000056a.pdf/what_explains_specialty_coffee_quality_scores_and_prices_a_case_study_from_the_cup_of_excellence_program.pdf). Caveat: the paper's text says "an extra meter in altitude is worth 0.3 point," but with a coefficient of 0.2907 that implies altitude is scaled (likely per 100 m). Read it as a per-unit effect, not per meter.
- Variety examples:
  - Disease-resistant cultivars "can produce quality coffees as good as those from the best traditional varieties" when environmental factors are optimal, i.e., environment and variety interact. — [_Experimental Agriculture_ 2009 abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/experimental-agriculture/article/abs/cup-quality-of-diseaseresistant-cultivars-of-arabica-coffee-coffea-arabica/BF69ADDB7C35EB83FDE4B3615DC87159)
  - Price-relevant variety effects differ across CoE studies: Pacamara only (Donnet 2008); Bourbon and Pacamara (Teuber & Herrmann 2012); Caturra and Pacamara positive, Typica negative (Traore 2018). — [Traore et al. 2018](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/18F08D9E4A9E6E03C46F9EF971ECBB8A/S1074070818000056a.pdf/what_explains_specialty_coffee_quality_scores_and_prices_a_case_study_from_the_cup_of_excellence_program.pdf)
- A 2023 review of 78 studies (_Agronomy for Sustainable Development_) assessed whether ecological quality (biodiversity, ecosystem functions) affects arabica and robusta quality. Only the abstract was read; specific conclusions were not verified. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13593-023-00874-z)
- A 2026 review frames quality as gene × environment × postharvest interactions: temperature, altitude and water "modulate gene expression and metabolic pathways," and shade and nutrient management "help steer these pathways." — [MDPI _Agriculture_ 16(7):739, 2026](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0472/16/7/739) (snippet only)

### Inferences

- Coffee terroir is real but **more local and more practice-dependent** than wine marketing implies. Slope aspect, yield load and post-harvest choices vary farm by farm and lot by lot. So "region" is a weak predictor, and the farm/lot (plus the producer's practices) is the meaningful unit. That strengthens the case for durable _producer/farm_ entities over region tags.
- Altitude is the one terroir variable with consistent evidence. It is also one of the most commonly disclosed fields, so coffee metadata over-indexes on the one variable that is easy to state.

### Gaps

- No good quantitative evidence on soil effects specifically (none surfaced; the 2021 review highlights inconsistency).
- No study found that separates producer skill (harvest and processing) from site (terroir) using repeated observations of the same farm. This is the coffee equivalent of wine's vintage-vs-vineyard decomposition.

## Q4. Why producer identity is often not disclosed: structure, aggregation, traceability

### Takeaway

The structural default is aggregation. Roughly 12.5M farms exist, about 95% of them under 5 ha, and cherry from many smallholders is pooled at washing stations, cooperatives and exchanges. Ethiopia offers a natural experiment: when the ECX (2008) stripped lot identity, export prices for non-traceable coffee fell by about 12 percentage points relative to traceable coffee. The estimated forgone revenue was **$280M, equal to 26% of farmers' coffee income**. Identity loss is therefore an economic cost, not just a data inconvenience.

### Cited Findings

- Coffee is grown on about **12.5 million farms** in more than 50 countries, and roughly **95%** are under 5 ha. — [Fairtrade Risk Map](https://riskmap.fairtrade.net/commodities/coffee)
- Sources disagree on smallholders' share of output:
  - 95% of 12.5M farms are under 5 ha and contribute **67%** of global production. — [Coffee Intelligence, Dec 2023](https://intelligence.coffee/2023/12/smallholder-coffee-farmer/); [Perfect Daily Grind](https://perfectdailygrind.com/2024/01/medium-farms-specialty-coffee/)
  - Enveritas-based figures: **60%** from farms under 5 ha, 19% from 5–50 ha, 21% from estates over 50 ha. — [CARTO/Enveritas, 2019](https://carto.com/blog/enveritas-coffee-poverty-visualization/); also "60%" in [Frontiers in Sustainable Food Systems 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sustainable-food-systems/articles/10.3389/fsufs.2022.808207/full)
  - Use "60–67%" and cite both.
- **Ethiopia ECX natural experiment (Leung 2014, Queen's University Development Discussion Paper 2014-04).** From December 2008, the ECX handled coffee "in a commodity fashion" that "eroded traceability." — [Leung 2014 PDF](https://cri-world.com/publications/qed_dp_249.pdf)
  - Using 699 export transactions, the price gap between traceable (producer) and ECX-sourced coffee widened by **12.0 percentage points** after ECX (95% CI 8.4–15.7).
  - Cumulative loss: **$280M**. Farmers capture 53% of export value, so a 14% fall in export value implies a **26% loss of farmers' coffee income**.
  - Before ECX, direct export never exceeded **15%** of volume.
  - Coffee was 32% ($823M) of Ethiopian exports in 2011, supporting 15 million workers.
- ECX grouped coffee only by region from 2008 to roughly 2017/2018. That "eliminated most roasters' and importers' ability to provide accurate information of the precise traceability" until reforms allowed washing stations and private exporters to sell outside the ECX. — [Mercanta/Coffee Hunter](https://coffeehunter.com/ethiopian-coffee-goes-fully-traceable-grant-rattray-gets-the-scoop-first-hand/); [Cabey Café](https://cabeycafe.com/knowledge/ecx/) (industry sources); [Ozone Coffee](https://ozonecoffee.co.uk/blogs/journal/ethiopia-coffee-trading-system)
- Even fully traced Ethiopian "lots" are typically washing-station lots, aggregating cherry from many smallholders. Lot-level provenance means "this washing station in this sub-region," not a single farmer. — [Ozone Coffee](https://ozonecoffee.co.uk/blogs/journal/ethiopia-coffee-trading-system)

### Inferences

- Non-disclosure has several distinct causes, worth separating in the post:
  1. **Physical aggregation.** No single producer exists for the lot (washing station, co-op blend). This is legitimate, but the _station_ is still a durable entity that could carry context.
  2. **Institutional stripping.** Exchanges such as ECX or grade-based trade remove identity.
  3. **Commercial protection.** Importers may withhold identity to protect sourcing relationships. **This motive is widely discussed in the trade, but no source verifying it was found in this session; treat it as a hypothesis.**
  4. **No persistent identifier.** Even when a name is disclosed, it is free text that varies by supplier and disappears with the lot. This matches Purveyors' catalog finding.
- The ECX result is the strongest quantitative evidence that buyers pay for identity/traceability itself, holding variety and grade roughly constant. It supports the "context has economic value" half of the thesis.

### Gaps

- No systematic survey was found of how often importers disclose producer names, or why they withhold them. Industry folklore about protecting relationships is unverified.
- No figure was found for the average number of smallholders contributing to a typical washing-station lot (it varies widely; commonly cited as hundreds in Ethiopia and Rwanda, but this is unsourced here).
- Leung 2014 is a discussion paper, not peer-reviewed; the sample is modest (699 obs).

## Q5. Direct trade / relationship coffee: does it improve quality and producer outcomes?

### Takeaway

The evidence is mixed and modest. The best-identified study (Hernandez-Aguilera et al., propensity score matching, 265 Colombian smallholders) found **no significant farm-gate price difference** for relationship-coffee participants. It did find better access to credit, more environmentally friendly practices and better business understanding. Other studies report higher prices for specialty/direct-trade sellers, but with weaker identification. Relationship benefits appear to come through stability, information and credit more than headline price.

### Cited Findings

- **Hernandez-Aguilera, Gómez, Rodewald, Rueda, Anunu, Bennett, (Schindelbeck), van Es** studied the Relationship Coffee Model (RCM), "long-term partnerships between coffee buyers and smallholder growers based on transparency, product quality and value sharing." Data: 265 Colombian smallholders, with propensity score matching. — [RePEc AAEA 2015 paper](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ags/aaea15/205650.html); published as "Quality as a Driver of Sustainable Agricultural Value Chains: The Case of the Relationship Coffee Model," _Business Strategy and the Environment_ 27:179–198 (2018) — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/bse.2009)
  - RCM participants "employ more environmentally-friendly resource management practices, have better understanding of the coffee business and are more optimistic about the future."
  - "**Although farm gate prices did not significantly differ between the two groups, RCM participants had increased access to credit.**"
  - The framework examines how geography, environment, practices and technology determine quality and therefore eligibility for RCM. Quality gates entry into the relationship rather than the relationship producing quality.
- A participatory action-research study in Colombia (2023): "On average, specialty coffee farmers received twice the FNC-defined market price," with "a clear trend towards specialty coffee and direct trade relationships among interviewed" farmers. — [ScienceDirect, 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S245229292300067X) (abstract snippet; small-sample qualitative design; selection not controlled)
- The SCTG is used by producers and exporters "as an alternative reference to the C price to begin conversations about pricing based on the specific kind of coffee they have, thinking about cup score, lot size, and origin" (Karl Wienhold). — [Daily Coffee News, Feb 2025](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2025/02/18/the-2024-specialty-coffee-transaction-guide-is-here/)
- SCTG overall median FOB: **$3.48/lb** over the last three harvest years (2021/22–2023/24), up $0.73 from the prior three. In 2023/24 the median was $3.50, with an IQR of $2.76–$4.68. — [2024 SCTG PDF](https://nzsca.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/2024TransactionGuideFinal-English.pdf)

### Inferences

- "Relationships improve outcomes" is **contested at the price level** and **better supported for non-price outcomes** (credit, practices, information). For the blog, the defensible claim is that relationships create _continuity of information_ (repeat purchases from a known producer), which is itself the context that accumulates. Avoid claiming direct trade reliably raises farm-gate price.
- The SCTG does not record relationship duration or repeat-buyer status. Even the best price transparency dataset has no persistent producer or relationship dimension, which mirrors Purveyors' finding.

### Gaps

- No quantitative study was found on whether multi-year buyer–producer relationships _raise cup quality over time_ (as distinct from selecting already-high-quality producers).
- The SCTG does not publish repeat-relationship price differentials. Earlier editions may have; not checked.

## Q6. Coffee Geographical Indications: what worked?

### Takeaway

Coffee GIs and trademarks are best documented as **legal and marketing wins**: Café de Colombia was the EU PGI in 2007, and Ethiopia's trademarks led to the 2007 Starbucks licensing deal. Kona and Jamaica Blue Mountain hold the highest single-origin retail premiums. Rigorous evidence that GIs raised _farmer_ income is thin. GIs protect a regional name, but a region is not a producer, so they do not solve producer-level context.

### Cited Findings

- **Café de Colombia** was recognized as a PGI under EU legislation in **2007**, the culmination of a reputation-protection strategy by the Federación Nacional de Cafeteros (founded 1927). — [IASC commons case study](https://iasc-commons.org/cs-colombian-growers-coffee/); [UK PGI specification PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/66584a667b792ffff71a8533/Cafe_de_Colombia.pdf)
- The FNC states the denomination of origin aims to "ensure that the premium of origin is recognized to producers of 100% Colombian Coffee," because "consumers are willing to pay a premium price for consistent and verifiable quality." — [Café de Colombia](https://cafedecolombia.com/en/denomination-of-origin/) (advocacy source, not evidence of a measured premium)
- The IASC case notes that GI producers must avoid over-producing because of "the limited number of consumers willing to pay a price premium for the GI products." — [IASC](https://iasc-commons.org/cs-colombian-growers-coffee/)
- A 2025 study (Caravaggio & Vaquero-Piñeiro, _Bio-based and Applied Economics_ 15(2):3–16) examines whether Café de Colombia GIs contribute to environmental conservation. Findings were not read. — [FUP](https://oaj.fupress.net/index.php/bae/article/view/17307)
- Teuber (2010): US online retail data show 100% Kona and Jamaica Blue Mountain as the most expensive single origins, and single origins overall earn significantly higher retail prices. — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/08974431003641612)
- **Ethiopia Trademarking & Licensing Initiative.**
  - The Ethiopian IP Office filed to register Harrar/Harar, Sidamo and Yirgacheffe as trademarks rather than GIs, seeking control over distribution. — [WIPO IP Advantage case](https://www.wipo.int/en/web/ip-advantage/w/stories/the-coffee-war-ethiopia-and-the-starbucks-story)
  - Ethiopia's rationale: high retail prices (Harar $24/lb in 2004, Sidamo $26/lb in 2005), but "only 5–10 percent comes to Ethiopia." — [WIPO presentation](https://www.wipo.int/edocs/mdocs/mdocs/en/wipo_res_dev_ge_09/wipo_res_dev_ge_09_www_130155.ppt)
  - Starbucks and Ethiopia signed a distribution, marketing and licensing agreement on **June 20, 2007**, ending the dispute. — [Oxfam via CSRwire](https://csrwire.com/press-release/oxfam-celebrates-win-win-outcome-for-ethiopian-coffee-farmers-and-starbucks/)
  - Ethiopia itself said results would be measured "over years… not in months." — [CSRwire, Aug 2007](https://csrwire.com/press-release/press-statement-29th-august-2007/)
- A blog claim that "Yirgacheffe farmer income roughly doubled in the year immediately following the settlement" — [TalkAboutCoffee](https://www.talkaboutcoffee.com/yrgacheffe-coffee-a-brand-or-an-origin-coffee.html) — is **unsourced and should not be used**. It also conflicts with the timing of ECX (Dec 2008), which, per Leung 2014, _depressed_ prices for non-traceable Ethiopian coffee shortly afterwards.

### Inferences

- Ethiopia ran two contradictory experiments within 18 months. It protected regional _names_ (trademarks, 2005–2007), then commoditized _lot identity_ through ECX (2008), which cost an estimated $280M. A protected regional name did not substitute for traceable lot identity. This is a strong narrative for the "metadata vs. durable context" thesis.
- GIs operate at the region level, roughly analogous to wine appellations. Wine's advantage is that the producer, estate and vintage sit _on top of_ the appellation as persistent entities. Coffee has regional names but rarely a persistent producer layer.

### Gaps

- No rigorous ex-post evaluation of farmer income from the Ethiopian trademark initiative was found.
- Kona's and Jamaica Blue Mountain's legal regimes (e.g., Kona blend labeling rules, the JBM certification mark) were not researched this session (search rate limits).
- No quantitative estimate was found of the Café de Colombia PGI price premium after 2007.

## Q7. Consumer and roaster evidence: does origin or producer information raise willingness to pay or perceived taste?

### Takeaway

Stated-preference studies consistently find positive WTP for origin indication, variety, elevation and certification labels, and one tasting experiment found that GI information shifts sensory evaluation. Evidence specific to _named-producer_ information, as opposed to origin labels, is thin. On the trade side, CoE buyers pay more for richer cupping descriptors (+0.74% per descriptor), showing that information content is priced.

### Cited Findings

- Liu, Chen & Chen (cited in a 2021 _Physiology & Behavior_ paper) found that "origin indication, variety, elevation at the point of origin, and sustainability certification labels have a positive effect on willingness to pay (WTP)." The citing paper, "A cup of black coffee with GI, please!", reports a tasting experiment on GI influence. — [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0031938421003589) (full text not accessible; effect sizes not verified)
- A 2026 coffee-fair choice study notes that "discrete choice experiments… consistently show that consumers are willing to pay a premium for credence attributes such as organic production, fair trade, and origin certification." — [ScienceDirect 2026](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2949824426002569) (abstract snippet)
- A cross-cultural choice experiment studied Spanish and Colombian consumer preferences for origin and specialty attributes (Fair Trade, sustainable, organic, gourmet). — [_Food Research International_ 2016](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0963996916301211) (abstract only; results not verified)
- The SCA ran eye-tracking experiments on coffee labels. One tested price, brand, variety, organic certification and origin; another tested price, SCA cupping score, altitude and roast level, with price included to estimate WTP. — [SCA News, issue 23 (2025)](https://sca.coffee/sca-news/25/issue-23/interpreting-attention) (search snippet; page returned 404 when fetched, so results not verified)
- "Virtual terroir" research argues that specialty consumers want origin and farming information in exchange for higher prices, citing WIPO 2017. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.586983/full) (snippet)
- B2B: in CoE auctions, each additional flavor descriptor is associated with **+0.74%** price, and buyers are advised to "refer to the cupping notes before making buying decisions" because scores are not comparable across countries. — [Traore et al. 2018](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/18F08D9E4A9E6E03C46F9EF971ECBB8A/S1074070818000056a.pdf/what_explains_specialty_coffee_quality_scores_and_prices_a_case_study_from_the_cup_of_excellence_program.pdf)

### Inferences

- That origin and credence information raises consumer WTP is **established in stated-preference work**, which carries the usual hypothetical-bias caveats. That _producer-name_ information specifically raises WTP or perceived taste is **plausible but under-evidenced** for coffee. The blog should not overclaim it.
- For Purveyors' audience (small and home roasters), the relevant buyer is the roaster. The evidence that roasters and auction buyers pay for richer descriptive information (descriptors, rank, origin) is stronger than the consumer evidence.

### Gaps

- No revealed-preference (actual sales) study was found showing that producer-named retail coffees sell at a premium over otherwise-equivalent unnamed single origins.
- No effect sizes were verified for the GI tasting experiment or the SCA eye-tracking study (paywall or 404).
- Home-roaster purchasing behavior around producer information appears unstudied.

## Cross-cutting notes for the report writer (summary of strength of evidence)

**Established**

- Score is priced, but rank, origin, variety, lot size and descriptors (symbolic attributes) explain as much or more of auction price (Donnet 2008; Wilson & Wilson 2014; Traore 2018).
- Altitude improves cup quality, and excessive light worsens it (73-study review).
- Smallholders dominate: ~12.5M farms, ~95% under 5 ha, ~60–67% of output.
- Removing traceability lowered Ethiopian export prices (~12 pp; $280M; 26% of farmer coffee income; Leung 2014, discussion paper).

**Contested or weak**

- Relationship trade raising farm-gate price (best PSM study: no significant difference; benefits in credit and practices).
- GI farmer-income effects.
- Producer-name WTP among consumers.
- Soil and temperature terroir effects.

**Unstudied (opportunities for Purveyors)**

- Persistence of producer quality across harvests.
- Producer fixed effects in price.
- Cross-supplier entity resolution of farms.
- The relationship-duration dimension in price benchmarks such as the SCTG.
