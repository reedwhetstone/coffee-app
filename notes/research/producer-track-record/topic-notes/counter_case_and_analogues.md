# Counter-case and blind spots: when coffee producer "story" informs, misleads, exploits, or substitutes for quality

Research date: 2026-10-02. Web search (Brave) was intermittently rate-limited (HTTP 429); I retried with varied queries. Items I could not verify from a fetched or clearly attributed source are listed under Gaps rather than filled from memory.

## 1. Sensory/expectation evidence: does origin/producer/story information change perceived taste? Useful context or bias?

### Takeaway

Yes, and the evidence is strong. Labels, price and descriptive information measurably change both stated liking and perception, even when the product is identical. The effects are largest when taste differences are ambiguous and when the label matches the taster's values. So story is not neutral context: it acts on the sensory experience itself. That argues for keeping verifiable attributes and blind sensory data separate from narrative.

### Cited Findings

- **Eco-label effect in coffee (Sörqvist et al., 2013, PLOS ONE).** Participants tasted two cups of identical coffee from the same brew. One cup was falsely described as "eco-friendly." Participants preferred its taste and were willing to pay more for it, at least those scoring high on sustainable-consumer attitudes (Exp. 1; n=44, 23 high and 21 low sustainability). High-sustainability consumers kept paying more for the "eco-friendly" coffee even after being told they had preferred the unlabeled cup (Exp. 2). The bias held when responses were anonymous, so it is not social desirability (Exp. 3). The authors conclude that eco labels "lead to a more favorable perceptual experience." — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0080719)
- The same paper's literature review notes that these information effects "distort the basic sensory and perceptual experience rather than biasing self-reported taste preferences" and are "particularly strong when objective taste is ambiguous." It cites brand-reveal effects on hamburgers, sodas and beer, and price effects on wine. — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0080719)
- **Price changes experienced pleasantness (Plassmann et al., 2008, PNAS).** Raising a wine's stated price increased reported flavor pleasantness and BOLD activity in the medial orbitofrontal cortex, a region thought to encode experienced pleasantness. — [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0706929105); [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2242704/)
- **Specialty coffee descriptors steer flavor perception (Brazil, Food Science and Technology / SciELO).** Q-Graders scored a fermented coffee at 85.15. In a CATA test with 101 consumers, the uninformed group noticed more caramel, while the group given information on the same coffee noticed more citric flavor. The authors conclude that information "can modify their sensory perception." They frame this as a positive for spreading specialty coffee information, which is the "useful context" reading. — [SciELO](http://www.scielo.br/j/cta/a/cSjz6CM9ScRVL6KHypQdyYy/?lang=en)
- **Brand information shifts coffee ratings.** A 2025 Taiwan study tested Starbucks and 7-Eleven Americanos under blind, correct-brand and incorrect-brand conditions (30 men, 30 women). Only the design was verified; result magnitudes were not fetched. — [SAGE Open](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/21582440251316087)
- Consumer-science literature notes that "consumers often like products that specialists consider to be of low quality," so lay and expert quality judgments diverge (snippet from LWT, 2020, "Do consumers perceive sensory differences by knowing information about coffee quality?"). Full text was blocked (403). — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0023643820317679)
- **Origin branding moves stated quality perception at scale.** Five months after Colombia's FNC launched the Juan Valdez campaign in 1959, the number of US consumers who said they recognized Colombian coffee as "excellent" reportedly rose 300%, and about 60% said they would pay more. This comes via Coffee Intelligence, a secondary source. — [Coffee Intelligence](https://intelligence.coffee/2022/07/the-fetishisation-of-coffee-farmers/)

### Inferences

- **Two readings are both defensible.** (a) Bias: story inflates perceived quality, so story-heavy listings can sell a weaker coffee as a better one. (b) Legitimate guidance: descriptors help tasters notice attributes that are really there (e.g., citric vs. caramel), much as tasting notes train perception. The difference is whether the information is accurate and checkable.
- **The B2B setting matters.** Small and home roasters buying green coffee are closer to "experts" than the consumers in these studies, but they are not immune: price effects and value-alignment effects work through the same mechanisms. Practical implication for Purveyors: show cupping scores and sensory notes separately from narrative fields, and record who cupped, when, and whether it was blind.
- **The eco-label result is a warning for "impact" stories specifically.** Moral framing raised both liking and willingness to pay, and buyers paid more even after learning they preferred the other cup. Story can therefore substitute for quality in the buyer's mind.

### Gaps

- I found no peer-reviewed study on whether a _named producer or farmer story_ (as opposed to eco/Fairtrade/brand/price labels) changes coffee taste ratings. This is a real gap in the evidence; extrapolating from adjacent label studies is an inference.
- I did not retrieve a Fairtrade-specific taste-expectation study for coffee (prompt suggestion); not verified.
- I found no evidence on whether trained Q-graders or roasters are less susceptible to producer-story effects.

## 2. Critiques of coffee storytelling: farmer-photo marketing, poverty narratives, cherry-picking, "farmwashing," and value capture

### Takeaway

The critique is well documented and partly quantified. Specialty differentiation and producer-centered marketing have _not_ raised the share of retail value reaching origin. Some studies find specialty segments return a _smaller_ share than mainstream coffee. Producer imagery is criticized as exoticizing, consent-light, and concentrated on a small set of celebrated farms.

### Cited Findings

- **Value distribution, mainstream baseline (Wienhold & Roberts, World Development 2025, summarized by the authors in DCN).** The 2015–2019 FOB share of retail prices across all coffee was 23.5%, roughly constant through the first quarter of the 21st century. — [Daily Coffee News](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2025/07/15/a-closer-look-at-value-distribution-in-specialty-coffee-markets/); [World Development abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305750X25001883)
- **Specialty does worse on share.** For low-end certified and uncertified specialty coffee, FOB prices were "little more than 13% of retail prices." Retail prices ran as much as four-fold higher while FOB prices were only marginally higher. — [DCN (Wienhold & Roberts)](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2025/07/15/a-closer-look-at-value-distribution-in-specialty-coffee-markets/)
- **Marginal capture.** Across differentiated specialty segments, people in exporting countries (including producers) "only absorbed between 7 and 16 cents of each additional dollar generated through higher prices." The authors add that quality-improvement efforts "help downstream profits and salaries more than they help those whose efforts and investments give rise to that improved quality." They also note "prolific references to specific coffee producers in the marketing of those coffees," meaning producer stories coexist with low value capture. A few companies come close to mainstream distribution outcomes. — [DCN](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2025/07/15/a-closer-look-at-value-distribution-in-specialty-coffee-markets/)
- The authors say fragmentation in specialty makes "consolidation leads to monopsony" an insufficient explanation. They point to "unequal access to information, inefficient pricing structures and institutional factors." Information asymmetry is therefore a named cause, which is relevant to a data platform. — [DCN](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2025/07/15/a-closer-look-at-value-distribution-in-specialty-coffee-markets/)
- **2026 Coffee Barometer (Panhuysen & de Vries, Ethos Agriculture, with Vocal, Conservation International, Solidaridad; funded in part by German BMZ):**
  - German retail example (via Le Basic): €9.71/kg retail; farmers received €2.05 gross; net income €0.41 after production costs. Unpaid family labor can add up to about 10% of the retail price.
  - Ground coffee at €8.06/kg returned about 24% of retail to farmers; capsules at €35.98/kg returned 6%.
  - Specialty differentiation does not automatically mean a better deal. The Barometer finds some specialty segments return a smaller share to producing countries than commodity markets.
  - Of 15 of the largest roasters and traders assessed, none disclosed pricing structures, contract durations, risk-sharing, or premiums paid. "Opacity, the evidence shows, is a choice."
  - Top-five trader share of green coffee trade rose from about 30% (2018) to an estimated 50% (2025).
  - Companies are increasingly replacing third-party certification with proprietary second-party schemes, which "eroded independent volume records."
  - Source for all of the above: [DCN, Inside the 2026 Coffee Barometer Part 2](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2026/07/14/inside-the-2026-coffee-barometer-part-2-what-companies-are-not-saying/)
- Producing countries' share of final retail value fell from about 30% in the early 1990s to about 10% today (ICO 2020, as cited in Coffee Barometer 2026 commentary). — [Comunicaffe](https://www.comunicaffe.com/matteo-borea-coffee-barometer-2026-sustainability-living-income). Note: the ~10% "producing countries" figure and the 23.5% "FOB share" measure different things and different baskets, so they should not be presented as contradictory.
- Barometer co-author Panhuysen: "If sourcing is justified on quality or differentiation, the real question is whether that premium reaches farmers in relation to their additional costs." He says smaller roasters are better placed to be transparent about pricing. — [DCN](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2026/07/14/inside-the-2026-coffee-barometer-part-2-what-companies-are-not-saying/)
- **Commodity fetishism to exoticization.** Coffee Intelligence (2022) summarizes the critique that farmer imagery creates "false expectations of what it means to live and work on a coffee farm." It quotes Dr. Gavin Fridell (co-editor, _The Fair Trade Handbook_): "There is an inherent coloniality to a kind of project like Fairtrade, that, at least in its imagery, it says that it is up to the consumer to fix this problem." Producer-owned alternatives cited include Pachamama Coffee Cooperative (farmer-owned roaster) and Nguyen Coffee Supply. — [Coffee Intelligence](https://intelligence.coffee/2022/07/the-fetishisation-of-coffee-farmers/)
- **Cherry-picking and concentration.** Coffee Intelligence (Jan 2026) argues that "a large share of specialty volumes in each producing country is sourced from a remarkably small circle of highly visible, competition-winning farms." Roasters "bid over the same handful of celebrated names" while marketing that suggests they are "uncovering hidden gems." It references Ted Fischer (SCA Re:co 2019) contrasting Cup of Excellence winners with neighbors producing similar quality. Context figures from the same piece:
  - GCP: 1,391,796 MT of "sustainable" purchases in 2023, more than 73% of reported purchases.
  - IISD: 43% of consumers say ethical options influence them.
  - Columbia CCSI (2021): in 8 of 10 producing countries, average coffee income is at or below the poverty line.
  - Source: [Coffee Intelligence](https://intelligence.coffee/2026/01/specialty-coffee-and-producers-success/)
- SCA _25_ magazine, Issue 12 (Ever Meister) examined "do good" storytelling: commodification, "upselling," story ownership, extraction, and exploitation. Cited via Coffee Intelligence; not fetched directly. — [SCA 25 Issue 12](https://sca.coffee/sca-news/25/issue-12/step-right-up)
- **Consent and naming.** Industry packaging guidance warns that "without explicit consent or compensation for using their image, brands can unintentionally exploit farmers." — [MTPak Coffee](https://mtpak.coffee/2024/11/images-farmers-on-coffee-bags/). Practitioners interviewed by Coffee Intelligence advise "We always name the producer… you'd be shocked how many people post photos of farmers" without naming them, and frame good practice as "education, not poverty porn." — [Coffee Intelligence 2025](https://intelligence.coffee/2025/04/highlight-staff-but-not-performative/)

### Inferences

- **Strongest objection to the thesis.** Coffee already has plenty of producer story. What it lacks is _commercial-terms_ transparency (price paid, contract length, premiums, who captured the margin). Adding more narrative context without price and terms data could worsen the "story substitutes for distribution" problem the Barometer and Wienhold & Roberts describe.
- **A defensible reframe for Purveyors.** "Context" should mean verifiable, buyer-useful facts (FOB/farmgate where disclosed, importer, lot size, process, variety with verification status, cupping provenance, harvest date, repeat-relationship history). Prose biography is not the goal. Story fields should carry provenance (who wrote it, whether the producer approved it).
- **The concentration critique applies to data platforms too.** A platform that ranks or features producers can amplify the "same handful of celebrated names" effect.

### Gaps

- I did not find a quantified study of how often roaster marketing uses producer photos or stories without consent or attribution.
- The term "origin washing" did not surface in reputable sources; "farmwashing" appears in a Medium essay ([Alvarado](https://anasofiaalvarado.medium.com/how-coffee-farmwashing-exploits-farmers-stories-and-livelihoods-464c223ce8b3)) but not in peer-reviewed or major-journalism sources I found.
- I did not fetch the primary 2026 Coffee Barometer PDF ([coffeebarometer.org](https://coffeebarometer.org/)). Figures come from DCN's reporting on it.

## 3. Producer data ownership, consent, and power (incl. EUDR geolocation)

### Takeaway

Farm-level data is increasingly required (EUDR) and commercially valuable. Practitioners and NGOs flag that it is usually controlled by buyers, certifiers, or financiers rather than farmers. Plot geolocation, identity, and price data carry real exposure and dependency risks. Consent and ownership design is an open problem, not a solved one.

### Cited Findings

- **EUDR status as of Oct 2026.** Regulation (EU) 2023/1115, as amended by (EU) 2025/2650, applies from 30 December 2026 for large/medium operators and 30 June 2027 for micro/small operators. The deforestation cut-off date (31 Dec 2020) never moved. The original application date was 30 Dec 2024, postponed twice. According to GreenCalculus (reviewed Sept 2026), a May 2026 Commission simplification package confirmed no further postponement and cut expected compliance costs by roughly 75% without removing core obligations. — [GreenCalculus](https://greencalculus.com/standards/eudr/); dates corroborated by [AGRINFO](https://agrinfo.eu/book-of-reports/eu-deforestation-regulation-eudr-clarifications-of-commodities-and-products/) and [Ivrify Data](https://ivrifydata.io/blog/eudr-deadline-30-december-2026). The "75%" and "May 2026 package" claims come from a single secondary source and should be checked against the Commission before publication.
- **What EUDR requires.** Geolocation for every production plot (a single point for plots under 4 ha; a polygon above 4 ha), legality proof, and a due diligence statement. Records are kept five years. — [Ethio Coffee](https://www.ethiocoffee.co/insights/eu-deforestation-regulation-ethiopian-coffee-compliance); [TraceX](https://tracextech.com/ethiopian-coffee-exporters-eudr/)
- GreenCalculus notes compliance "depends on data held by farmers rather than by the company that must file." — [GreenCalculus](https://greencalculus.com/standards/eudr/)
- Many smallholders farm "on land without formal property titles, digital cadastral records, or mapping tools." — [Coolx](https://coolx.earth/en/blog/eudr-and-coffee-a-practical-guide-for-exporters-and-importers)
- **Who controls the data.** Solidaridad (Dec 2025) says digital tools for farmer data "are often controlled by external actors—buyers, certifiers, or financiers—leaving farmers without true ownership or control over their own data, thereby creating dependency." Its Beyco pilot with seven Colombian cooperatives tested farmer-controlled data for traceability and carbon insetting. — [Solidaridad](https://www.solidaridadnetwork.org/publications/empowering-coffee-farmers-with-digital-data-management/)
- An East African Fine Coffees Association traceability platform document (Project ROBUST, July 2026) emphasizes "the need to balance traceability with data protection and farmer data sovereignty." — [EAFCA](https://eastafricacoffee.org/document/show/15974)
- **The 2026 Barometer frames data control as power.** "Who defines and controls the data? Independent certification with audited volumes, or proprietary systems where companies set their own indicators and reporting boundaries?" CSRD/CSDDD "pull data requirements upstream," so even mid-sized roasters will be asked for traceability data. — [DCN](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2026/07/14/inside-the-2026-coffee-barometer-part-2-what-companies-are-not-saying/)
- EUDR compliance (mapping, geolocation, recordkeeping, training, digital tools) adds real costs that smallholders often carry first (roaster blog paraphrasing Solidaridad; low-tier source). — [Pascucci](https://pascuccicoffee.com/blogs/blog/coffee-farming-economics-what-farmers-really-earn)

### Inferences

- **Specific risks for a context-capture platform:**
  - Exposing producer-level prices can weaken a producer's negotiating position with other buyers, or a buyer's with competitors.
  - Plot coordinates plus a name and photo can enable security risks in some regions. This is an inference; I found no documented incidents.
  - Stories scraped from importer pages are third-party content that the producer may never have approved.
- **Design implication:** store the source and consent status of each context field. Prefer importer- or producer-published facts. Do not publish plot-level geolocation. Treat price data as aggregated or disclosed-by-party.

### Gaps

- I found no documented incident of harm from public producer geolocation or price disclosure in coffee.
- I found no legal analysis of GDPR applicability to EUDR smallholder data in the sources fetched (some farmers are natural persons, so it plausibly applies; not verified).

## 4. Verification problems: certification effectiveness, coffee fraud/mislabeling, wine-fraud parallels

### Takeaway

Claims that consumers and buyers pay for are frequently unverified or false: Kona volumes, Gesha variety identity, Darjeeling volumes, and old Burgundy. Certification impact on farmer income is mixed and context-dependent. Context is only as valuable as its verification layer.

### Cited Findings

- **Kona.** The original 2019 Lanham Act complaint lab-tested 19 products sold as Kona that allegedly contained little or no Kona coffee. It stated: "only 2.7 million pounds of authentic green Kona coffee is grown annually, over 20 million pounds of coffee labeled as 'Kona' is sold at retail." By Sept 2023, settlements totaled $33.4M across 21 defendants (including Costco, Walmart, Amazon, Kroger). An economist valued injunctive labeling provisions at $81.2M over five years. — [Daily Coffee News](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2023/09/27/federal-judge-approves-final-12-million-settlement-in-kona-labeling-case/). Plaintiffs' counsel describes final approval with defendants agreeing to pay "more than $41 million." — [Lieff Cabraser](https://www.lieffcabraser.com/2023/10/court-grants-final-approval-to-settlement-in-kona-coffee-litigation/). Conflict: $33.4M (DCN) vs. >$41M (Lieff Cabraser). The likely explanation is differing scope or timing, but this is unverified; cite both or the conservative figure. Case: Corker et al. v. Costco, No. 2:19-cv-00290 (W.D. Wash.). — [Top Class Actions](https://topclassactions.com/lawsuit-settlements/consumer-products/food/kona-class-action-over-false-labeling-settled-for-6-15m/)
- **Gesha/variety mislabeling.** WCR DNA fingerprinting of more than 2,500 samples (Journal of AOAC International, 2020) found only 39% of 88 samples identified as Gesha were genetically Gesha. The recently released Marsellesa showed 91% conformity. Likely causes are cross-pollination and poorly organized nursery networks, so much of this is unintentional misidentification rather than fraud. Caveat: WCR sells the DNA test ($130/sample) and has a stated interest. — [Daily Coffee News](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2020/04/08/genetic-testing-reveals-the-true-nature-of-coffee-varieties/)
- A 2026 Frontiers in Plant Science study describes an Ecuadorian farm that bought uncertified seed labeled Sidra (USD 100/kg) and Gesha (USD 500/kg) and found morphological/genetic discordance. It notes "many producing countries lack the certification infrastructure necessary to guarantee this identity." — [Frontiers / PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13409818/)
- A 2026 systematic review (27 studies, 2015–2025) finds Arabica-for-Robusta substitution remains widespread, detection below 1–5% adulteration is hard, and "geographical origin discrimination [is] limited for proximate regions." — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2949824426004088). Abstract highlights only.
- **Certification effectiveness (Fairtrade).** In identical surveys of 738 cooperative smallholders in Ethiopia, India, and Nicaragua (propensity-score matching):
  - Ethiopia: Fairtrade farmers fared _worse_ than non-certified farmers on yield and income, and received prices well below the Fairtrade minimum (weight-equivalent).
  - India: certified farmers had yield, price, and net-revenue advantages.
  - Nicaragua: higher net revenue but no significant household-income effect.
  - The authors cite seven meta-analyses that "commonly conclude that certification has rather mixed results," with most studies finding no difference between certified and non-certified producers.
  - Source: [Frontiers in Sustainable Food Systems 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sustainable-food-systems/articles/10.3389/fsufs.2021.716904/full)
- **Other income findings.**
  - India panel: certified farmers' income was 17% higher on average ([ARER 2016 via RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/agrerw/v45y2016i03p436-458_00.html)).
  - Central Kenya: because of overcertification, certified sales are at most one-third of volume, and the expected income effect is under 10% of household income ([World Development 2015](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305750X15002028)).
  - NBER digest: Fair Trade certification lifts incomes in coffee communities, including for non-coffee workers ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/digest/apr18/fair-trade-certification-lifts-incomes-coffee-communities)). Snippet-level only; magnitudes not verified.
- **Rainforest Alliance.**
  - A Rwanda study found certification positively affected environmental characteristics and gave economic benefits to most farmers ([AEPP 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/aepp.13307)).
  - A southwestern Ethiopia case study found parts of certified coffee forests deforested or degraded, with RA requirements "not uniformly applied" ([Frontiers in Environmental Science 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/environmental-science/articles/10.3389/fenvs.2023.1193242/full)).
  - Status: contested and context-dependent.
- **Shift to self-certification.** The 2026 Barometer reports companies moving from third-party to proprietary second-party schemes, eroding independent records. — [DCN](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2026/07/14/inside-the-2026-coffee-barometer-part-2-what-companies-are-not-saying/)
- **Wine-fraud parallel.**
  - Rudy Kurniawan was sentenced to 10 years for counterfeit wine ([FBI](https://www.fbi.gov/news/stories/rare-wine-dealer-sentenced-in-counterfeiting-scheme); page fetch blocked, title and snippet only). BBC reported he sold over $20M of fake wine over more than a decade ([BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news/business-28697721)).
  - The Guardian quotes an expert suspecting "80% of the Burgundy allegedly from before 1980 is counterfeit." This is one expert's opinion, not a measurement ([Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/global/2016/sep/11/the-great-wine-fraud-a-vintage-swindle)).
  - Kurniawan was caught partly because Domaine Ponsot identified bottles of vintages it never made. That detail comes from a Reddit summary, so it is low-confidence; verify before use.
  - Lesson: wine's rich context did not prevent fraud. Detection came from producer-held records and identity.

### Inferences

- **Wine is not uniformly "better."** Its context layer is rich, but fraud flourishes exactly where story and scarcity are high and verification is weak (old Burgundy). The wine lesson for coffee is about the _institutions_ that make labels mean something (appellation law, producer records), not about richer narrative.
- **Variety claims are the weakest fields in green-coffee listings.** A platform should label variety as "claimed" vs. "DNA-verified" rather than present it as fact.
- **Certification badges are context with a mixed evidence base.** Presenting them as proof of farmer benefit would overclaim.

### Gaps

- Jamaica Blue Mountain mislabeling: not researched to a verifiable source in this pass; gap.
- I found no reliable quantitative estimate of overall specialty green-coffee origin mislabeling rates.
- Fairtrade's own most recent impact reports were not fetched.

## 5. Adjacent industries that turned producer/place context into durable infrastructure: what made it trustworthy?

### Takeaway

Context became trustworthy where five things held:

1. A collective body owned the name and rules (Comté CIGC, Scotch regs, Darjeeling GI).
2. An independent or state verifier audited compliance (Bureau Veritas/INAO for Comté; HMRC for Scotch; USDA-ARS genetics for heirloom cacao).
3. Blind sensory evaluation was separated from the story (HCP tasting panel).
4. Identity persisted over time (distillery names, PDO specs, genetic databases).
5. Governance also addressed value distribution (Comté supply caps, producer-owned fruitières).

Where verification lagged the name's value (Darjeeling, Kona), counterfeit volume swamped the real thing.

### Cited Findings

- **Comté PDO (cheese).**
  - Fruitières (cooperative cheesemaking) date to the 13th century; one of the first was founded in 1253 in Deservilliers, Doubs. Historic Paris market records show Comté priced well above similar cheeses. — [European Commission](https://agriculture.ec.europa.eu/farming/geographical-indications-and-quality-schemes/geographical-indications-food-and-drink/comte-pdo_en)
  - The specification requires Montbéliarde or Pie rouge de l'Est cows, fodder mainly from the defined area, at least 120 days of maturing, and 30–55 kg wheels. — [European Commission](https://agriculture.ec.europa.eu/farming/geographical-indications-and-quality-schemes/geographical-indications-food-and-drink/comte-pdo_en)
  - Since 1958 the CIGC (Comité Interprofessionnel) has brought farmers, cheesemakers, and affineurs into one body; about 14,000 people are employed regionally. — [culture magazine, Jan 2025](https://culturecheesemag.com/travel/comte-cheese/)
  - Governance rules beyond flavor: an annual milk production cap that "prevents farm consolidation," a minimum forage area per cow, and fruitières that can collect milk only within a 16-mile radius. About 4,500 dairy farmers "typically own the cheesemaking facilities they sell to." Comté has around 140 cheesemaking facilities. — [culture magazine](https://culturecheesemag.com/travel/comte-cheese/)
  - Supply regulation for Comté (along with Beaufort, Reblochon, and others) is formally recognized under EU dairy market rules. — [European Commission dairy page](https://agriculture.ec.europa.eu/farming/animal-products/milk-and-dairy-products_en)
  - CIGC acts as the ODG under INAO supervision, and Bureau Veritas (INAO-accredited) audits farms, fruitières, and affineurs. This comes from a Grokipedia snippet, a low-reliability source; verify against INAO or CIGC before publication. — [Grokipedia](https://grokipedia.com/page/Comt%C3%A9_cheese)
  - A 2019 EU-funded case study (Husson, Bellassen et al.) found Comté "substantially outperforms its reference products in price premium, operating margin, food miles, employment, bargaining power and generation renewal." This is from the abstract snippet; the full text was bot-blocked. — [HAL](https://hal.science/hal-02401239)
  - Volume: 64,065 tons in 2015 (MDPI 2022 snippet). — [MDPI Animals](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-2615/12/10/1299)
- **Scotch Whisky Regulations 2009 (SI 2009/2890).**
  - Five mandatory category terms (Single Malt, Single Grain, Blended Malt, Blended Grain, Blended).
  - Single Malt must be bottled in Scotland, and all Scotch wholly matured in Scotland.
  - A distillery name may not be used as a brand on any Scotch "not wholly distilled in the named distillery."
  - Protected regions: Highland, Lowland, Speyside, Islay, Campbeltown. "Pure Malt" is banned, and there are clear rules on age statements.
  - HMRC is designated as the verification authority.
  - Sources: [BeverageDaily](https://www.beveragedaily.com/Article/2009/11/24/New-rules-offer-protection-for-Scotch-Whisky/); [legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2009/2890/made); [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en/insights/topics/whisky-law-insights/2024/scotch-whisky-labelling-and-bottling)
- **Heirloom Cacao Preservation Fund (HCP).**
  - Founded with USDA-ARS and the Fine Chocolate Industry Association; a 501(c)(3). It reports 24+ Heirloom designations, 12 countries, and 3,200+ farmers. — [HCP About](https://www.hcpcacao.org/en/about)
  - Process: the HCP Lab at Guittard "blindly processes all submissions" for an international tasting panel. Applicants receive a detailed report, then USDA-ARS performs a site visit and genetic analysis to map the trees' DNA and preserve them in a database. — [HCP news (2015)](https://www.hcpcacao.org/en/news/first-ever-heirloom-cacao)
  - The panel consists of experts from six countries, each with at least 15 years' experience ([HCP FAQ snippet](https://www.hcpcacao.org/hcp-faqs.html)). Genetic results "are shared directly with the farmer" ([HCP apply page snippet](https://www.hcpcacao.org/apply-to-the-hcp.html)).
  - Key design feature: flavor merit is established blind _before_ story or designation, and identity is anchored genetically.
- **Darjeeling tea GI.** Actual annual production is about 8–9 million kg, while historically more than 40 million kg has been sold globally under the Darjeeling name. — [Firstpost](https://www.firstpost.com/lifestyle/why-darjeeling-tea-is-famous-globally-the-story-behind-the-champagne-of-teas-14017601.html); [Golden Tips Tea](https://www.goldentipstea.com/blogs/all/what-does-gi-certified-darjeeling-tea-really-mean-for-consumers). These are widely repeated estimates with no primary measurement located; treat as indicative. Darjeeling has a GI and a Tea Board logo, but the counterfeit gap shows a registered name without enforcement and traceability does not hold.
- **Kona as the coffee counter-example.** Hawaii's Kona growers lacked a certification mark that would prohibit other sellers from using the geographic name (Top Class Actions, citing the case), so litigation substituted for governance. — [Top Class Actions](https://topclassactions.com/lawsuit-settlements/consumer-products/food/kona-class-action-over-false-labeling-settled-for-6-15m/)

### Inferences

- **What generalizes to coffee data:**
  - Persistent identifiers for producers, farms, and lots that survive importer changes and years. This mirrors distillery names and PDO registers.
  - Separate fields for claim and verification (who verified, how: DNA, audit, blind cup).
  - Blind sensory data as the anchor, with story layered on top (HCP model).
  - Producer-side governance and consent over how a name or story is used (Comté/CIGC, Pachamama).
- **What does not generalize:** PDO-style supply control and legal name protection need state and collective institutions that a private data platform cannot supply. Purveyors can _record_ and _surface_ verification status but cannot _be_ the verifier without independent processes.
- **Wine and cheese worked because the producer or collective owned the identity.** In coffee, the importer usually owns the listing and the story. That asymmetry is the core blind spot of a "capture context" thesis.
