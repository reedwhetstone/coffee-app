# Buffett-style fundamental investing and the economics of soft information, as an analogy for producer context in green coffee

Research date: 2026-10-02. All Buffett quotes below were checked word-for-word against the original letter text downloaded from berkshirehathaway.com in this session, unless a line is flagged otherwise. Academic findings were checked against the paper text or abstract, again unless flagged.

## Q1. What did Buffett actually say about how he evaluates businesses?

### Takeaway

Buffett's stated method is not "hard data plus vibes." It is a disciplined reading of **long, demonstrated track records**: returns on capital, capital intensity and owner earnings. He adds a small set of qualitative judgments: whether the moat is durable, whether management is able and honest, and whether the business is simple enough to sit inside his circle of competence. He explicitly discounts forecasts and treats stock prices as a mood indicator, not a source of information. The qualitative judgment works as a filter on durability. It does not replace the numbers.

### Cited Findings

**Circle of competence (1996 letter):** "What an investor needs is the ability to correctly evaluate selected businesses. Note that word 'selected': You don't have to be an expert on every company, or even many. You only have to be able to evaluate companies within your circle of competence. The size of that circle is not very important; knowing its boundaries, however, is vital." The same passage continues: "To invest successfully, you need not understand beta, efficient markets, modern portfolio theory, option pricing or emerging markets." — [Berkshire 1996 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1996.html)

**Investor's goal (1996):** "Your goal as an investor should simply be to purchase, at a rational price, a part interest in an easily-understandable business whose earnings are virtually certain to be materially higher five, ten and twenty years from now... If you aren't willing to own a stock for ten years, don't even think about owning it for ten minutes." — [1996 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1996.html)

**Moat, earliest letter uses:**

- 1986: GEICO's low-cost position is "a kind of moat that protects a valuable and much-sought-after business castle." Buffett says Bill Snyder "continually widens the moat by driving down costs still more, thereby defending and strengthening the economic franchise." The evidence he cites is quantitative: the expense ratio fell from 24.1% to 23.5%. — [1986 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1986.html)
- 1993, on Coke and Gillette: "The might of their brand names, the attributes of their products, and the strength of their distribution systems give them an enormous competitive advantage, setting up a protective moat around their economic castles." — [1993 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1993.html)

**Moat, canonical definition (2007):** "A truly great business must have an enduring 'moat' that protects excellent returns on invested capital. The dynamics of capitalism guarantee that competitors will repeatedly assault any business 'castle' that is earning high returns... Business history is filled with 'Roman Candles,' companies whose moats proved illusory and were soon crossed... A moat that must be continuously rebuilt will eventually be no moat at all." The same passage contrasts a star surgeon's practice ("The partnership's moat will go when the surgeon goes") with the Mayo Clinic, whose moat will endure "even though you can't name its CEO." — [2007 letter (PDF)](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2007ltr.pdf)

- Note that the moat is defined in terms of a hard metric, "returns on invested capital." The qualitative part is the judgment about whether those returns will last.

**Moat as an explicitly subjective test (2007):** Berkshire evaluates its big stock holdings in two ways. "The first test is improvement in earnings... The second test, more subjective, is whether their 'moats' – a metaphor for the superiorities they possess that make life difficult for their competitors – have widened during the year." Buffett also says they do not judge investments "by what their market prices do during any given year." — [2007 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2007ltr.pdf)

**See's Candies (2007):**

- "We bought See's for $25 million when its sales were $30 million and pre-tax earnings were less than $5 million. The capital then required to conduct the business was $8 million... Consequently, the company was earning 60% pre-tax on invested capital."
- By 2007, sales were $383 million and pre-tax profit $82 million, with only $32 million reinvested since 1972. Cumulative pre-tax earnings reached $1.35 billion.
- Unit volume grew from 16 million to 31 million pounds, "a growth rate of only 2% annually." Buffett credits a "durable competitive advantage, built by the See's family over a 50-year period."
- Source: [2007 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2007ltr.pdf)
- For the analogy: See's is the clearest case of a qualitative asset (brand affection, built by a family over decades) showing up as a quantitative signature (return on capital and pricing power with almost no volume growth).

**Coca-Cola and "The Inevitables" (1996):** "Companies such as Coca-Cola and Gillette might well be labeled 'The Inevitables.'... no sensible observer... questions that Coke and Gillette will dominate their fields worldwide for an investment lifetime." In the same passage he warns: "Leadership alone provides no certainties: Witness the shocks some years back at General Motors, IBM and Sears." — [1996 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1996.html)

- 1993: "The competitive strengths of a Coke or Gillette are obvious to even the casual observer of business. Yet the beta of their stocks is similar to that of a great many run-of-the-mill companies." Buffett uses this to argue that price volatility (beta) is the wrong measure of risk: "It is better to be approximately right than precisely wrong." — [1993 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1993.html)

**Owner earnings (1986 appendix):**

- Definition: "(a) reported earnings plus (b) depreciation, depletion, amortization, and certain other non-cash charges... less (c) the average annual amount of capitalized expenditures for plant and equipment, etc. that the business requires to fully maintain its long-term competitive position and its unit volume."
- Then: "Our owner-earnings equation does not yield the deceptively precise figures provided by GAAP, since (c) must be a guess – and one sometimes very difficult to make. Despite this problem, we consider the owner earnings figure, not the GAAP figure, to be the relevant item for valuation purposes." Buffett quotes Keynes: "I would rather be vaguely right than precisely wrong."
- Source: [1986 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1986.html)
- This matters for the analogy: Buffett's preferred number has a judgment call (maintenance capex) built into it. The line between hard and soft runs through the middle of his formula.

**"Price is what you pay; value is what you get":** The verified source is the 2008 letter: "Long ago, Ben Graham taught me that 'Price is what you pay; value is what you get.' Whether we're talking about socks or stocks, I like buying quality merchandise when it is marked down." — [2008 letter (PDF)](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2008ltr.pdf)

- Buffett himself credits the line to Graham, so calling it "Buffett's line" is a minor misattribution. I did not find the exact phrase in Graham's own published writing (see Gaps).

**Wonderful company at a fair price (1989, "Mistakes of the First Twenty-five Years"):** "It's far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price. Charlie understood this early; I was a slow learner. But now, when buying companies or common stocks, we look for first-class businesses accompanied by first-class managements." — [1989 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1989.html)

- Buffett credits the insight to Munger.
- The same section says: "Good jockeys will do well on good horses, but not on broken-down nags. Both Berkshire's textile business and Hochschild, Kohn had able and honest people running them... when a management with a reputation for brilliance tackles a business with a reputation for bad economics, it is the reputation of the business that remains intact."
- In other words, management quality is necessary but subordinate to business economics.

**Acquisition criteria (printed in letters such as 1987 and 1989):**

1. "demonstrated consistent earning power (future projections are of little interest to us, nor are 'turnaround' situations)"
2. "businesses earning good returns on equity while employing little or no debt"
3. "management in place (we can't supply it)"
4. "simple businesses (if there's lots of technology, we won't understand it)"
5. "an offering price"

Source: [1987 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1987.html); [1989 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1989.html)

- The wording is "of **little** interest," not "of no interest," as it is sometimes paraphrased.

**Mr. Market (1987, credited to Graham):** Market quotes come from a partner with "incurable emotional problems." When euphoric, "he can see only the favorable factors affecting the business"; when depressed, he "can see nothing but trouble ahead." — [1987 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1987.html)

- This is the verified primary-source basis for "don't fixate on ticker shapes." Buffett's objection to price-watching is about **emotion and noise**, not specifically about technical analysis.

### Inferences

- The author's framing ("Buffett leans on hard data but evaluates the full picture") is broadly right but should be sharpened. Buffett's qualitative judgments are almost all about **durability**: will today's high return on capital persist? The evidence he cites for durability is usually a long quantitative history, such as See's 35 years of returns or GEICO's expense ratio.
- The coffee analog of "demonstrated consistent earning power" is therefore a **multi-season track record per producer**: consistency of quality, volumes, price and relationship tenure. A richer one-off story is not the analog. Producer "context" is most Buffett-like when it is longitudinal.
- "Future projections are of little interest" cuts against narrative-heavy producer marketing: aspirational claims about a farm's plans are exactly the kind of soft information Buffett discounted.
- Circle of competence maps onto buyers: a roaster does not need full context on every origin, but needs to know where their judgment ends. That is a case for context that is selective and decision-relevant, not exhaustive.
- Even Buffett treats management as secondary to business economics ("good jockeys... broken-down nags"). By analogy, a great producer in a structurally hard position (climate exposure, logistics, a thin export channel) can still be a fragile supplier. Context should include structural position, not only the producer's character.

### Gaps

- **Phil Fisher's "scuttlebutt" and "15% Fisher, 85% Graham":** I did not verify either against a primary source this session. Scuttlebutt comes from Fisher's _Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits_ (1958). The "85% Graham / 15% Fisher" line is widely attributed to a 1969 Forbes profile of Buffett, but I could not confirm the original text. Treat it as secondary until checked.
- **"I realized technical analysis didn't work when I turned the charts upside down and didn't get a different answer":** this is widely circulated but I found no primary source. Treat it as likely apocryphal; Mr. Market (1987) is the safe citation instead.
- I did not locate a verified Buffett quote on management "candor" or on reading annual reports for this note. The 1983 appendix on economic goodwill (See's) is a separate page from the main 1983 letter and was not retrieved.
- I did not find "Price is what you pay; value is what you get" in Graham's published writing. Buffett credits Graham, but the exact Graham source is unconfirmed.

## Q2. Is Buffett's edge explainable by quantifiable quality factors (Frazzini, Kabiller & Pedersen)?

### Takeaway

Largely yes. Once you control for exposure to safe, cheap, high-quality stocks, Berkshire's alpha becomes statistically insignificant. A systematic, screen-based "Buffett-style" portfolio performs comparably to Berkshire. Much of the "full picture" Buffett read qualitatively turned out to be capturable in hard data after the fact. His edge was **recognizing early which qualities mattered**, applying cheap leverage, and sticking with it.

### Cited Findings

- **Abstract, NBER WP 19681 (2013):** "the alpha becomes insignificant when controlling for exposures to Betting-Against-Beta and Quality-Minus-Junk factors. Further, we estimate that Buffett's leverage is about 1.6-to-1 on average. Buffett's returns appear to be neither luck nor magic, but, rather, reward for the use of leverage combined with a focus on cheap, safe, quality stocks." — [NBER w19681](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w19681/w19681.pdf)
- **Published version:** _Financial Analysts Journal_ 74(4):35–55, 2018. It states leverage at "about 1.7 to 1, on average." Cite the 1.7 figure for the FAJ version and 1.6 for the 2013 working paper. — [CFA Institute / FAJ abstract](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2018/faj-v74-n4-3); [SSRN 3197185](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3197185)
- **How "quality" is defined:** in the QMJ factor (Asness, Frazzini & Pedersen 2013), quality means companies that are "profitable, growing, safe and have high payout." All of these are hard, accounting-based measures. — [NBER w19681](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w19681/w19681.pdf)
- **Replication result:** "we create a portfolio that tracks Buffett's market exposure and active stock-selection themes, leveraged to the same active risk as Berkshire. We find that this systematic Buffett-style portfolio performs comparably to Berkshire Hathaway." — [NBER w19681](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w19681/w19681.pdf)
- **The authors' interpretation:** "Buffett's genius thus appears to be at least partly in recognizing early on, implicitly or explicitly, that these factors work, applying leverage without ever having to fire sale, and sticking to his principles." They quote Buffett: "Ben Graham taught me 45 years ago that in investing it is not necessary to do extraordinary things to get extraordinary results" (1994 letter, as quoted in the paper). — [NBER w19681](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w19681/w19681.pdf)
- **Selection vs. management:** Berkshire's publicly traded stocks outperform its wholly owned private businesses, "suggesting that Buffett's returns are more due to stock selection than to his effect on management." — [NBER w19681](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w19681/w19681.pdf)
- **Cheap financing:** 36% of Berkshire's liabilities were insurance float, at an average cost below the T-bill rate. — [NBER w19681](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w19681/w19681.pdf)

### Inferences

- **The key nuance for the post:** "the full picture" was not ineffable. Much of what Buffett judged qualitatively (durable moats, honest conservative management) left a quantitative fingerprint in profitability, stability, low leverage and payout. Academics could later score it.
- Applied to coffee: the gap is partly a **data-structure problem, not a "numbers can't capture it" problem**. Some producer context can be hardened, such as tenure of buyer relationships, lot-to-lot consistency, delivery reliability, repeat-buyer rate, and price history relative to the C-market. A platform that hardens these first-movers' signals is doing what Frazzini et al. did to Buffett.
- **The counterpoint the author should not miss:** once a quality signal is hardened and widely available, it becomes a factor and stops being an edge. Soft context gives an edge precisely because few buyers have it. Publishing it to everyone shifts its value from individual edge to market efficiency (fairer pricing, less "lemons" discounting). The post should decide which of the two it is selling.
- Leverage and float were half of Buffett's result. By analogy, information alone does not create returns for a coffee buyer without the capital, patience and contract structures to act on it (forward contracts, multi-year relationships).

### Gaps

- I did not retrieve the full FAJ 2018 text to compare its regression coefficients with the 2013 WP; only the headline leverage figures were compared.
- There is no academic study that quantifies how much of Buffett's private-business acquisition skill rests on soft (interview-based) judgment.

## Q3. Hard vs. soft information in finance (Petersen 2004; Stein 2002; Liberti & Petersen 2019)

### Takeaway

Hard information is quantitative, context-free, and separable from the person who collected it, so it travels cheaply through hierarchies and scales. Soft information is text, opinion and judgment. Its meaning depends on context and on who collected it, so it degrades when passed up a chain or squeezed into a score. Standardizing on hard information lowers cost but loses accuracy, and it invites gaming: the target learns which numbers matter.

### Cited Findings

- **Definitions (Liberti & Petersen):** "Hard information is quantitative, easy to store and transmit in impersonal ways, and its information content is independent of its collection." — [Liberti & Petersen, NBER w25075 (2018); published _Review of Corporate Finance Studies_ 8(1):1–41, 2019](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25075/w25075.pdf); [RCFS](https://academic.oup.com/rcfs/article/8/1/1/5220773)
- **Soft information:** "Information that is difficult to completely summarize in a numeric score, where context matters, and where it is difficult to separate the collection of the information from its use is what we call soft information." It "is often communicated as text... opinions, ideas, rumors, economic projections, statements of management's future plans, and market commentary." — [w25075](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25075/w25075.pdf)
- **A score is not automatically hard information:** "One can always create a numerical score from soft information, for example by creating an index of how honest a potential borrower is. This in and of itself doesn't make the information hard. Your interpretation of a 3 must be the same as mine." — [w25075](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25075/w25075.pdf)
- **Who collects it:** "Soft information must be collected in person, and the information collector and the decision maker are often the same person." This is why relationship lending is built on soft information (Berger & Udell 1995). — [w25075](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25075/w25075.pdf)
- **Hardening means loss:** "A numeric score can always be created, the issue is how much valuable information is lost in the process. We call this process the hardening of information." — [w25075](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25075/w25075.pdf)
- **The cost–accuracy tradeoff:** a decision maker using hard information is "trading off the lower cost of collecting and processing the information with potential loss in accuracy." Hard information also removes discretion: "The advantage of hard information is it can remove the loan officer's discretion." — [w25075](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25075/w25075.pdf)
- **Historical hardening, 19th-century credit agencies:** these agencies turned local merchants' personal knowledge into published scores. But "The credit rating bureaus were either unable or unwilling to quantify and include all of the soft information they held into their reported credit scores," and the detailed private reports "[were] better at predicting bad outcomes (business failures) than the published credit ratings" (citing Carruthers). — [w25075](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25075/w25075.pdf)
- **When hard data replaces soft data, models break:** as securitization lengthened the chain from loan originator to investor, "the performance of credit models deteriorated," citing Rajan, Seru & Vig 2015. "When loans are securitized using only information that can be passed on to investors (i.e., hard information), there is little incentive for the lenders to collect soft information." — [w25075](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25075/w25075.pdf)
- **Stein (2002), _Journal of Finance_ 57(5):1891–1921:** "A decentralized approach—with small, single-manager firms—is most likely to be attractive when information about projects is 'soft' and cannot be credibly transmitted. In contrast, large hierarchies perform better when information can be costlessly 'hardened' and passed along inside the firm." Stein applies this to bank consolidation reducing small-business lending. — [RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v57y2002i5p1891-1921.html)
- **Liberti & Mian (2009), as summarized by Liberti & Petersen:** "greater hierarchical distance is associated with less reliance on soft information and more on hard information." Personal interaction between loan officers and approvers "helps mitigate the effects." — [w25075](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25075/w25075.pdf)
- **Petersen (2004):** the original working paper "Information: Hard and Soft." Liberti & Petersen (2019) is its "significantly revised version." — [w25075 title note](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25075/w25075.pdf)

### Inferences

- **The green coffee chain is a hierarchy in Stein's sense.** The chain runs producer → wet mill / cooperative → exporter → importer → roaster, and each handoff is a "hierarchical distance." Soft knowledge about the producer predictably drops out, and what survives is the hard fields: origin, process, score, price. This gives the author's thesis a rigorous mechanism, not just a lament.
- **A cupping score is hardened soft information.** It is a sensory judgment compressed into a number. Liberti & Petersen's test ("your interpretation of a 3 must be the same as mine") is exactly the calibration problem with cross-importer scores. This is a strong, non-clichéd point for the post.
- **The credit-bureau history is a direct precedent.** The published score lost information that the private detailed report kept. The detailed report predicted failures better.
- **The Rajan/Seru/Vig mechanism is a risk the author may be missing.** If marketplaces rank and price lots only on hard fields, sellers lose the incentive to collect or convey soft context, and they may optimize the visible fields instead. Context fields would need structure (or verification) to avoid the same fate.
- **"Relationship coffee" (direct trade) is the analog of relationship lending.** It works because the collector and the decision maker are the same person. This suggests the realistic goal for a data platform is to **lower the cost of transmitting** soft information, through structured fields, provenance and attributable notes, not to eliminate the relationship.

### Gaps

- I did not retrieve Rajan, Seru & Vig (2015, _JFE_, "The failure of models that predict failure") directly; the characterization comes via Liberti & Petersen.
- I found no coffee-specific empirical study of soft-information loss along the green supply chain this session.

## Q4. Do intangibles and narrative matter for value, and where are the limits?

### Takeaway

Reported financial statements explain a much smaller share of market value than they did in the mid-20th century, because value has shifted to intangibles that accounting does not capture. Text can be measured and does move prices, but much of the narrative signal is sentiment that later reverses, not information about fundamentals. Narrative is real, but it is also a source of noise and bubbles.

### Cited Findings

- **Lev & Gu, _The End of Accounting_ (Wiley, 2016):** earnings and book value explained roughly 80–90% of market value in the 1950s–60s, versus about 50% recently, per secondary reports. — [Accounting Today, 2016](https://www.accountingtoday.com/news/the-end-of-accounting); [Value Research summary](https://www.valueresearchonline.com/stories/46235/the-end-of-accounting/)
  - Secondary sources disagree on the recent figure: Fortune reports "as low as 40%" and frames it as explaining "gains and losses in equities" (returns rather than levels). — [Fortune, 2016](https://fortune.com/2016/09/22/accounting-broken-baruch-lev/)
  - Use "roughly half" with care and cite the book directly if possible.
- **Tetlock (2007), _Journal of Finance_ 62(3):1139–1168:** using a daily Wall Street Journal column, "high media pessimism predicts downward pressure on market prices followed by a reversion to fundamentals." Results are "consistent with theoretical models of noise and liquidity traders, and are inconsistent with theories of media content as a proxy for new information about fundamental asset values." — [RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v62y2007i3p1139-1168.html)
- **Loughran & McDonald (2011), _Journal of Finance_ 66(1):35–65, "When Is a Liability Not a Liability?":** almost three-fourths of the words that the general-purpose Harvard dictionary codes as negative are not negative in financial contexts. They built finance-specific word lists. — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v66y2011i1p35-65.html) _(The abstract text did not load this session; the "three-fourths" figure is from my knowledge of the published abstract. Verify before quoting.)_
- **Shiller, "Narrative Economics," _AER_ 107(4):967–1004 (2017):** "The human brain has always been highly tuned toward narratives, whether factual or not, to justify ongoing actions, even such basic actions as spending and investing... Narratives 'go viral' and spread far." He treats the Great Depression and the 2007–09 recession as partly results of popular narratives. Expanded into the book _Narrative Economics_ (Princeton UP, 2019). — [RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v107y2017i4p967-1004.html)

### Inferences

- Lev & Gu support the author's direction: value increasingly sits in things the standard numbers miss. Tetlock and Shiller supply the necessary caution.
- **Tetlock's "reversion to fundamentals" is the finance version of a producer-story premium that may not survive the cup.** Narrative can move price without moving quality.
- **Loughran & McDonald show that soft information can be hardened usefully, but only with a domain-specific vocabulary.** For coffee, generic sentiment ("sustainable," "family farm") is the Harvard-dictionary mistake. Context needs a coffee-specific schema.
- Buffett's own stance is consistent with this: he valued intangibles (brand, franchise) but distrusted projections and market narratives (Mr. Market).

### Gaps

- _The End of Accounting_ was not consulted directly; the exact R² series and its methodology (levels vs. returns) are unverified. This is why the 40% and 50% figures conflict.
- No coffee-specific evidence was gathered on whether producer-story premiums persist after accounting for cup quality.

## Q5. Information economics for products: search, experience and credence goods; lemons; verification institutions

### Takeaway

Origin, farming practice and labor conditions are mostly **credence attributes**: the buyer cannot verify them even after roasting and tasting. Markets for credence attributes need reputation, third-party certification, or traceability institutions. Without them, quality uncertainty drives out good sellers (lemons) or invites false claims. Certifiers themselves are imperfect, so the institution matters as much as the data field.

### Cited Findings

- **Nelson (1970), "Information and Consumer Behavior," _Journal of Political Economy_ 78(2):311–329:** distinguishes search goods (quality checkable before purchase) from experience goods (quality learned only by use). — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jpolec/v78y1970i2p311-29.html) _(Abstract not displayed; the classification is the paper's well-established contribution.)_
- **Darby & Karni (1973), "Free Competition and the Optimal Amount of Fraud," _Journal of Law & Economics_ 16(1):67–88:** adds credence qualities, which cannot be evaluated even after use, and analyzes the resulting incentive for fraud. — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jlawec/v16y1973i1p67-88.html) _(Abstract not displayed; summary from the standard literature.)_
- **Akerlof (1970), "The Market for 'Lemons': Quality Uncertainty and the Market Mechanism," _QJE_ 84(3):488–500:** includes a section titled "Counteracting institutions" (p. 499), covering guarantees, brand names and licensing. — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/qjecon/v84y1970i3p488-500..html)
- **Dranove & Jin (2010), "Quality Disclosure and Certification: Theory and Practice," _Journal of Economic Literature_ 48(4):935–963:** asks "why don't sellers voluntarily disclose through a process of 'unraveling'" and "when we rely on certifiers to act as the intermediary of quality disclosure, do certifiers necessarily report unbiased and accurate information?" — [RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/jeclit/v48y2010i4p935-63.html)

### Inferences

**Mapping coffee fields onto the taxonomy:**

- Price, origin country and listed process are search attributes, but only if truthful.
- Cup quality is an experience attribute. It can be verified by sample roasting, which is why the cupping score is the one context-rich signal the market already hardened.
- Specific farm, producer identity, farming practice, labor conditions and price paid to the producer are credence attributes. A roaster cannot taste them.

**What this implies the author may be missing:**

1. "More context" is not enough. For credence attributes, **context without verification is cheap talk** (Darby & Karni). The value is in provenance, attribution and verification: who asserted this, when, and on what evidence. That is the institution Akerlof's "counteracting institutions" and Dranove & Jin's certifiers supply.
2. Unraveling logic: if good producers can credibly disclose, missing context becomes a negative signal. A platform that makes disclosure cheap and verifiable could make silence informative.
3. Certifiers can be biased or captured (Dranove & Jin), so a data platform acting as an information intermediary takes on certifier-style credibility obligations.

**Combined with Q2:** the Buffett analogy works best if framed as "the market prices the experience attributes and is blind to the durability attributes." Buffett paid for durability, and durability in a supplier shows up as relationship continuity, consistency across harvests, and economic sustainability for the producer. Those are partly measurable over time.

### Gaps

- Coffee-specific empirical work on certification premiums and on the effectiveness of traceability/verification (e.g., Fairtrade/organic premium studies, Cup of Excellence auction data) was out of scope for this note and not gathered.
- The full abstracts of Nelson (1970) and Darby & Karni (1973) did not render on RePEc. The citations are correct, but the summaries come from established literature rather than text fetched this session.
